How to read this

Each row pairs a machine-checkable predicate over per-cell statistics, for example slow.p3s4.heldout < slow.p4s4.heldout, with the mechanism and control the model recorded before the data could answer. p and s name an (order, stages) cell, the leading word picks the cost model, and heldout in a predicate is that cell elite's error on the held-out set, which selects elites and so is not a clean test set.

Verdicts come from code, never from the model. Once every cell a predicate names holds min_samples records, the predicate is evaluated against those cells' running statistics. d is Cohen's d; below 0.2 the verdict is inconclusive whichever way the comparison went, so a weak effect cannot be claimed. A predicate that names an empty cell is inconclusive too, because absence of evidence never refutes. n is the smallest record count among the named cells, and c is the cycle the verdict landed on (c123+ means proposed at cycle 123 and still open).

×N means the model posed that predicate N times over the run. The row shows the newest verdict and lists the id of every posing; mixed beside the verdict means those posings did not all get the same one.

Cortex-M0+ fixed-point constant multiplication collected 2026-09-23 11:52 CT, epoch 2, cycle 240

Arm specifies two implementation options for the Cortex-M0+ multiplier. MULS computes the low 32 bits of a 32-by-32-bit product and takes either one cycle with the fast array multiplier or 32 cycles with the small iterative multiplier. The choice is made by the chip implementer, so cycle models must use the selected microcontroller’s documentation rather than assuming that every Cortex-M0+ has the same multiplication cost. For Q15 operands, the exact signed product fits in 32 bits; an arithmetic right shift by 15 then performs the binary rescaling. ARMv6-M arithmetic right shift copies the sign bit and discards low bits, which implements floor division by a power of two for two's-complement values and therefore directly matches floor rounding.

  • Cortex-M0+ MULS returns the least-significant 32 bits of a 32-by-32-bit product.
  • The configurable multiplier is either a one-cycle array implementation or a 32-cycle iterative implementation.
  • The iterative multiplier can be abandoned and restarted when an interrupt is taken.
  • A signed Q15-by-Q15 product fits exactly in a signed 32-bit intermediate.
  • ASR by 15 supplies floor-rounded Q15 rescaling, including for negative products.
  • CSD represents constants with digits from {-1, 0, 1} and no adjacent nonzero digits.
  • CSD minimizes the number of nonzero signed digits, but it does not necessarily produce the minimum-depth or minimum-operation adder graph.
  • Shift-add constant multiplication replaces each nonzero signed digit with a shifted add or subtract.

Quantized adaptive RK control under hard timing limits collected 2026-09-23 11:02 CT, epoch 2, cycle 216

PI and digital-filter step controllers use present and previous error information to suppress the oscillatory step sequences produced by memoryless controllers. The cited control literature reports greater robustness, smoother step sequences, and improved computational stability at little or no additional function-evaluation cost. These results assume an error signal in the asymptotic step-size/error regime. In Q15 arithmetic, the embedded difference may quantize to zero near the resolution floor; floor rounding also gives every discarded fractional result a nonpositive quantization error. Consequently, controller ratios, powers, logarithms, saturation, zero-error handling, and minimum-step rules must be specified as integer algorithms rather than treated as transparent translations of real-arithmetic formulas.

  • Embedded pairs obtain a solution and local-error estimate from the same stage evaluations.
  • Bogacki, Shampine 3(2) is lower-stage and lower-order than Dormand, Prince 5(4), favoring different accuracy-versus-work regimes.
  • Dormand, Prince explicitly targets small fifth-order principal truncation terms and offers formulas with extended absolute-stability regions.
  • PI and digital-filter controllers use error history to smooth step changes and improve closed-loop robustness.
  • Adaptive rejection makes execution time data-dependent unless retries or total work are explicitly bounded.
  • FSAL reuse applies across a successful step boundary and can be lost when a trial step is rejected or otherwise invalidated.
  • Q15 can collapse a sufficiently small embedded error estimate to zero, requiring an explicit controller policy.
  • Floor rounding is biased downward, unlike round-to-nearest, and repeated stage and controller quantization can accumulate that bias.

Parameterized RK tableau searches collected 2026-09-23 10:13 CT, epoch 2, cycle 192

The literature treats Runge, Kutta design as constrained optimization: choose a stage count and order, enforce Butcher order conditions, then optimize remaining tableau freedom for a secondary objective. RK-Opt supports explicit, implicit, diagonally implicit, and low-storage classes; it can minimize the leading truncation-error coefficient or maximize the SSP coefficient, constrain selected stability-polynomial coefficients, and use multiple starting points. RootedTrees.jl provides rooted-tree generation of RK order conditions, while published low-order-through-sixth-order work explicitly studies optimal choices of free parameters.

Recent work also optimizes performance measured on representative problems instead of only formal local-error constants. A 2025 study searches four free parameters of an eight-stage, sixth-order family with particle-swarm optimization, minimizing global error over several step lengths and reporting that parameter bounds affect stability and truncation-error outcomes. For a fixed-cycle Q15 search, the literature therefore supplies two complementary templates: enumerate or numerically solve order-constrained parameter families, and score their remaining degrees of freedom using an implementation-relevant objective. The cited optimization systems formulate coefficients as real-valued unknowns, so a finite Q15 tableau search requires its own coefficient-domain and cycle-budget constraints.

  • Order conditions define a constrained manifold rather than a unique tableau when free parameters remain.
  • RK-Opt can minimize leading truncation error or maximize the SSP coefficient for a prescribed stage count and order.
  • Stability-polynomial constraints can be imposed separately from nonlinear RK order conditions.
  • Multistart and derivative-free searches address nonlinear coefficient spaces and local minima.
  • Rooted-tree software automates generation and checking of RK order conditions.
  • Problem-trained global-error objectives can select different free parameters than local-error minimization.
  • Coefficient bounds can materially change the resulting stability intervals and error constants.
  • Real-coefficient optimization results are candidate generators, not direct Q15 tableau specifications.

Rounding effects in fixed-point Runge, Kutta integration collected 2026-09-23 09:22 CT, epoch 2, cycle 168

Fixed-point ODE studies report a characteristic error floor: reducing the timestep initially lowers discretization error, but eventually increases or stagnates total error because more quantized operations are accumulated. Deterministic truncation or floor-style rounding introduces signed, non-zero-mean errors that can accumulate coherently. Hopkins et al. found this behavior in explicit fixed-step solvers for neuron ODEs and reported substantially lower arithmetic error with stochastic rounding; their experiments also found that six residual/random bits were sufficient for their stochastic multiplier and that input dither was a cheaper, though less consistently effective, alternative. The broader stochastic-rounding survey traces this idea to early fixed-point ODE computations and explains that unbiased stochastic rounding changes systematic drift into zero-mean noise.

  • Deterministic floor rounding is biased downward, unlike round-to-nearest or unbiased stochastic rounding.
  • In reduced precision, smaller timesteps can worsen results because the number of rounding events grows approximately as the inverse timestep.
  • Runge, Kutta heat-equation analysis found round-to-nearest stagnation and global rounding error proportional to O(u/Δt) before stagnation.
  • The same analysis found zero-mean leading errors under stochastic rounding and substantially weaker accumulation.
  • Fixed-point neuron-ODE experiments found stochastic rounding more accurate and robust than truncation and round-to-nearest across several explicit solvers.
  • Those neuron experiments found six residual/random bits sufficient for the tested stochastic-rounding multiplier.
  • Input dither can approximate some benefits of stochastic rounding with less computation, but its effectiveness is problem-dependent.
  • Arm's Cortex-M0 Q15 multiply implementation forms a 32-bit product, shifts it right by 15 bits, and saturates to the signed 16-bit Q15 range.

Cost structure of implicit RK families on Cortex-M0+ collected 2026-09-23 00:08 CT, epoch 2, cycle 144

SDIRK methods solve stages sequentially. Their common diagonal coefficient gives each Newton linearization the same matrix form, so a factorization can be reused across stages when the Jacobian and step size are held fixed. Fully implicit Radau IIA instead couples all stage unknowns; straightforward simplified Newton therefore produces a larger block system. Transformations and splittings can reduce Radau's factorization cost, sometimes to one state-sized factorization, but retain stage transformations, block substitutions, and convergence-dependent iterations. Literature on inexact simplified Newton identifies the nonlinear and linear solves as the principal implementation bottleneck. Work on fixed finite Newton counts shows that specially constructed generalized RK schemes can preserve the parent formula's order and linear stability, but this is a designed property rather than a general guarantee obtained by truncating any implicit solver after an arbitrary number of iterations.

Rosenbrock methods remove nonlinear iteration by linearizing the stages in the method definition. Each stage requires a right-hand-side evaluation and a linear solve; with a common diagonal gamma, all stages can reuse one factorization of I-h*gamma*J. Rosenbrock-W methods additionally permit approximate Jacobians. This makes their operation schedule more deterministic than convergence-tested Newton implementations, although Jacobian formation, factorization, and triangular solves remain costs. On Cortex-M0+, Arm documents one-cycle integer adds and shifts, but MULS may take either 1 or 32 cycles depending on the configured multiplier; loads and stores also have implementation-dependent timing. Consequently, Q15 cycle estimates must target the actual core implementation and explicitly count coefficient products, scaling shifts, memory traffic, Jacobian work, and linear-solver arithmetic. The consulted numerical literature analyzes real or floating-point arithmetic and does not establish accuracy or stability guarantees for Q15 floor rounding, so those effects require separate finite-precision analysis.

  • SDIRK stages are sequential nonlinear solves, but a common diagonal can permit reuse of one linear-system factorization.
  • Radau IIA couples all stages and has a larger native Newton system.
  • Radau transformations and splittings trade extra stage algebra for fewer or smaller factorizations.
  • Rosenbrock schemes replace nonlinear stage iteration with prescribed linear solves.
  • A common Rosenbrock diagonal permits one factorization to serve every stage of a step.
  • Rosenbrock-W methods allow an approximate Jacobian, subject to their own order conditions.
  • Arbitrarily stopping Newton after a fixed count does not generally preserve the parent RK method's properties.
  • Fixed-iteration schemes with preserved order and stability exist when constructed and analyzed specifically for that iteration rule.

Production ODE integrator architecture collected 2026-09-17 16:35 CT, epoch 2, cycle 48

CVODE is not a Runge, Kutta implementation: it uses variable-order, variable-step Adams, Moulton formulas for nonstiff systems or BDF formulas for stiff systems. Its state interface is N_Vector; the serial implementation contains a length, ownership flag, and pointer to a contiguous scalar array. CVODE applies a weighted RMS norm with weights 1/(rtol·|y_i|+atol_i), retries failed steps at a smaller size, and jointly varies step size and method order. Documented controller constants include a 1/6 safety factor in the principal step-size estimate, maximum ordinary growth of 10, earliest-step growth up to 10^4, and suppression of changes below a ratio of 1.5. These libraries therefore supply three distinct production patterns relevant to a fixed-cycle, fixed-point search: dense stage-buffered embedded RK with RMS acceptance, compile-time-generic RK with a maximum-component test, and history-buffered multistep integration with weighted RMS control. Their adaptive controllers and floating-point tolerance formulas require additional policy decisions when cycles are fixed and Q15 floor rounding introduces a non-negligible error quantum.

  • SciPy solve_ivp defaults to RK45 with rtol 1e-3 and atol 1e-6.
  • SciPy explicit RK accepts steps using an RMS norm of componentwise scaled embedded error.
  • SciPy RK step multipliers use safety 0.9 and bounds 0.2 to 10.
  • Boost.Odeint separates state storage, iteration algebra, and scalar operations through templates.
  • Boost.Odeint supports fixed-size std::array states and internally maintains reusable temporary buffers.
  • Boost.Odeint's default controlled-RK error reduction is a maximum over scaled component errors.
  • CVODE uses Adams or BDF multistep formulas rather than Runge, Kutta formulas.
  • CVODE's serial N_Vector stores a pointer to contiguous data together with length and ownership metadata.

Long-run roundoff, truncation balance in low-precision ODE solvers collected 2026-09-17 14:15 CT, epoch 2, cycle 24

Classical finite-precision analysis gives the global error of an order-p fixed-step ODE method as O(h^p + u/h): truncation error decreases with the step size h, while a worst-case roundoff contribution grows with the number of steps. Probabilistic models with independent, zero-mean local errors predict milder accumulation, commonly proportional to u/sqrt(h). Experiments and analysis for RK4 and other solvers show that unstable dynamics can amplify these perturbations and eventually produce distributions of trajectories rather than a single reliably convergent path. A posteriori analysis likewise finds accumulated roundoff inversely proportional to the square root of the step size and concludes that finite precision imposes an eventual accuracy limit.

  • Reducing the step size eventually increases total error once accumulated roundoff dominates truncation error.
  • The conventional worst-case balance is O(h^p + u/h) for an order-p method.
  • Zero-mean probabilistic models can reduce step-count growth from linear to approximately square-root behavior.
  • Floor rounding is directed and biased, so zero-mean cancellation assumptions used in probabilistic analyses do not apply directly.
  • In Q15, the representable spacing is 2^-15; unlike floating point, this is an absolute spacing across the usable range.
  • Fixed-point experiments found GCC round-down behavior and quantized constants detrimental to ODE accuracy.
  • Keeping products and intermediate values at higher precision before the final quantization reduces arithmetic error.
  • Long-time error depends on the ODE's perturbation amplification as well as on the RK order and local quantization size.

Embedded RK pairs and fixed-point step control collected 2026-09-08 21:39 CT, epoch 1, cycle 2608

Bogacki, Shampine introduced an embedded 3(2) Runge, Kutta pair whose shared stages provide a third-order update and a second-order error estimate. The commonly used scheme has four stages and the earliest-same-as-last property, allowing the final derivative of an accepted step to be reused as the earliest derivative of the next step. Dormand and Prince constructed embedded 5(4) pairs with seven stages, emphasizing small principal fifth-order truncation terms and extended absolute-stability regions. These results show that nominal stage count alone does not determine recurring cost: derivative reuse, accepted-step rate, coefficient arithmetic, and stability-limited step size also matter. They therefore give no general ordering between the archive cells in H-703 through H-709 without cell-specific tableaux and cycle measurements.

Budgeted low-precision RK and energy behavior collected 2026-09-08 21:05 CT, epoch 1, cycle 2600

Recent mixed-precision Runge, Kutta research treats arithmetic precision and stage evaluation placement as separate design variables. A 2026 study of Bogacki, Shampine 3(2) found that mixed precision can retain much of the high-precision accuracy, but low precision did not reduce the number of function evaluations enough to offset slower operations in the tested setting. Work on reduced-precision fixed-point ODE solvers also shows that rounding mode materially affects trajectory error: stochastic rounding outperformed deterministic round-to-nearest in the reported neuron-model experiments. Because floor rounding is directional rather than unbiased, classical order and stage count alone do not characterize its accumulated error.

Structure preservation introduces another axis beyond classical order. Explicit pseudo-energy-preserving RK schemes have been constructed with energy-preservation order higher than their classical order; tests reported smaller errors than same-order RK methods over moderately long Hamiltonian simulations. Thus, comparisons among four-, five-, and six-stage archive cells require coefficient-level measures of quantization sensitivity and invariant drift, not only nominal order. For cycle hypotheses, Arm identifies the Cortex-M0+ technical reference manual as the source for instruction timing, while Zephyr exposes direct cycle-difference measurements; measured compiled paths are therefore needed because equal stage counts need not imply equal instruction, memory-access, or right-hand-side costs.

  • Arithmetic precision and RK stage placement can be optimized independently.
  • Low precision does not automatically reduce function-evaluation count.
  • Rounding mode can materially change fixed-point ODE trajectory error.
  • Floor rounding can introduce systematic directional error.

Cortex-M0+ fixed-point multiplication costs collected 2026-09-08 20:35 CT, epoch 1, cycle 2592

Arm documents two implementation-time multiplier choices for Cortex-M0+: a fast single-cycle array multiplier and a smaller 32-cycle iterative multiplier. Both execute MULS, which returns the low 32 bits of a 32-by-32-bit product. The core timing table lists register addition, subtraction, and immediate or register shifts as one-cycle instructions, while loads can take one or two cycles depending on the implementation. Consequently, a cost model that assigns one universal price to multiplication does not represent all Cortex-M0+ devices. For signed Q15 operands, the full product fits within 31 significant bits, so MULS followed by ASRS #15 can implement the usual arithmetic-shift floor scaling without needing a 64-bit product.

Constant multiplication can instead be expressed as shifts plus additions or subtractions. Canonical signed-digit encoding minimizes the number of nonzero signed digits, but it does not necessarily produce the shortest possible addition chain; adder-graph methods can reuse intermediate expressions and sometimes improve on direct CSD expansion. GCC explicitly distinguishes Cortex-M0/M0+/M1 targets with the small iterative multiplier and may replace multiplication with add/subtract/shift sequences. These findings support treating fast-multiplier, slow-multiplier, and constant-specific shift-add costs separately when comparing Runge-Kutta cells: stage count alone does not determine cycles, because coefficient encodings, reused partial products, constant materialization, scaling shifts, and register spills can materially change the arithmetic cost.

  • Cortex-M0+ offers either a one-cycle or a 32-cycle MULS implementation.
  • MULS returns the least-significant 32 bits of a 32-by-32-bit product.
  • Basic register adds, subtracts, and shifts are listed as one-cycle operations.
  • ASRS supplies sign-preserving right shifts suitable for floor-style power-of-two scaling.
  • A signed Q15-by-Q15 product fits in the available 32-bit MULS result.
  • CSD reduces nonzero signed digits but is not always the optimal addition chain.
  • Adder graphs can share partial products across several constant multiplications.
  • GCC has distinct small-multiplier Cortex-M0+ tuning that favors shift-add replacements.
