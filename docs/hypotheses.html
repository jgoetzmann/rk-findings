How to read this

Each row pairs a machine-checkable predicate over per-cell statistics, for example slow.p3s4.heldout < slow.p4s4.heldout, with the mechanism and control the model recorded before the data could answer. p and s name an (order, stages) cell, the leading word picks the cost model, and heldout in a predicate is that cell elite's error on the held-out set, which selects elites and so is not a clean test set.

Verdicts come from code, never from the model. Once every cell a predicate names holds min_samples records, the predicate is evaluated against those cells' running statistics. d is Cohen's d; below 0.2 the verdict is inconclusive whichever way the comparison went, so a weak effect cannot be claimed. A predicate that names an empty cell is inconclusive too, because absence of evidence never refutes. n is the smallest record count among the named cells, and c is the cycle the verdict landed on (c123+ means proposed at cycle 123 and still open).

×N means the model posed that predicate N times over the run. The row shows the newest verdict and lists the id of every posing; mixed beside the verdict means those posings did not all get the same one.

Long-run roundoff and truncation error in quantized ODE solvers collected 2026-09-24 18:06 CT, epoch 2, cycle 1128

ODE solvers commonly show a V-shaped error curve as the step size decreases: truncation error initially falls, but eventually quantization or roundoff dominates because more steps and arithmetic operations inject more numerical error. Fixed-point experiments confirm that implementation details, including intermediate-variable ordering and coefficient quantization, can materially change the result. Runge, Kutta stage residuals can also be amplified within a step, and this internal amplification depends on the particular algebraic implementation, not just the Butcher tableau.

Probabilistic analyses obtain square-root-of-step-count growth when per-step roundoff is approximately independent and zero-mean. Systematic errors instead accumulate linearly; published Runge, Kutta studies identify rounded coefficients and solver termination effects as sources of such drift. Floor rounding is intrinsically one-sided except at exactly representable values, so the zero-mean random-walk model does not apply without additional symmetry or randomization. Fixed-point ODE experiments report accumulating errors from truncated constants and arithmetic, while stochastic rounding substantially reduces error in tested low-precision solvers. Backward-error analysis can explain bounded long-time errors when a numerical method preserves a nearby modified invariant, but coefficient quantization or biased arithmetic may destroy the structural conditions on which that conclusion depends.

  • Reducing the step size eventually increases roundoff exposure because the number of steps grows.
  • Zero-mean independent local errors predict root-N accumulation; biased errors can produce N-scale drift.
  • Floor rounding generally introduces a signed bias and therefore conflicts with the usual Brouwer-law assumptions.
  • Quantized Runge, Kutta coefficients can create systematic local error even when arithmetic noise is otherwise unbiased.
  • Internal stage-error amplification depends strongly on the implemented Runge, Kutta representation.
  • Fixed-point solver accuracy is sensitive to constant conversion, scaling, operation ordering, and intermediate precision.
  • Stochastic rounding removes deterministic directional bias in expectation and improved accuracy in published fixed-point ODE experiments.
  • Backward-error conclusions about long-time invariant behavior require preservation of the relevant discrete structure.

A fixed iteration cap makes execution more bounded but is not generally equivalent to solving the original implicit RK equations. SUNDIALS documents that fixed-point iterations avoid Jacobian-based linear solves but can converge more slowly or diverge, while Newton iterations are more robust for very stiff problems; its default maximum is three nonlinear iterations. Research has constructed generalized RK formulas that deliberately retain order and stability after a prescribed finite number of Newton-like iterations, showing that these properties require method-and-iteration design rather than arbitrary early termination. Cortex-M0+ constant-multiply costs collected 2026-09-24 15:43 CT, epoch 2, cycle 1056

Arm documents two implementation options for the Cortex-M0+ MULS instruction: a single-cycle array multiplier and a 32-cycle iterative multiplier. MULS returns only the low 32 bits of a 32×32 product; ordinary signed Q15 operands nevertheless have a product representable within 32 bits. Cortex-M0+ also provides single-cycle add, subtract, and immediate-shift instructions, while arithmetic right shift preserves the sign bit. For an in-range Q15 product, shifting right by 15 therefore implements division by 2^15 with floor behavior for negative nonmultiples, without a rounding correction.

Canonical signed-digit encoding represents a constant as signed powers of two, with the minimum number of nonzero digits and no adjacent nonzero digits. Constant multiplication can consequently be implemented as shifts plus additions or subtractions. This can materially change tableau cost on a Cortex-M0+ configured with the 32-cycle multiplier, but offers much less scope against a single-cycle multiplier; register moves, coefficient loads, spills, and instruction-fetch effects also remain. Thus stage count alone does not fix arithmetic cost: the five-stage and four-stage RK cells must be compared using their actual coefficient CSD weights, reuse opportunities, generated instruction sequences, and the multiplier variant assumed by the cycle model.

  • Cortex-M0+ permits either a 1-cycle or 32-cycle MULS implementation.
  • MULS produces the least-significant 32 bits of a 32×32 product.
  • Signed Q15-by-Q15 products fit in a signed 32-bit intermediate.
  • Arithmetic right shift naturally realizes floor division by powers of two for signed two's-complement values.
  • CSD has minimal nonzero-digit count and forbids adjacent nonzero digits.
  • A CSD coefficient maps to shifted operands combined by adds and subtracts.
  • Extra RK stages do not imply a fixed cycle increment when coefficient implementations differ.
  • Cycle comparisons must identify the concrete MCU multiplier variant and generated code.

Embedded RK pairs and quantized step control collected 2026-09-24 14:51 CT, epoch 2, cycle 1032

Bogacki, Shampine is an explicit embedded 3(2) pair: the second-order estimate controls error while the third-order result advances the solution. Dormand, Prince is an embedded 5(4) family commonly implemented with error control on the fourth-order estimate and advancement with the fifth-order result. Current implementations also exploit stage reuse and bound successive step-size changes; the lower-stage Bogacki, Shampine pair has a smaller per-step evaluation burden, while Dormand, Prince provides higher formal order at greater stage and coefficient cost.

PI and digital-filter controllers use current and previous error information to smooth step-size sequences and improve computational stability. Reference implementations calculate normalized error norms, powers or roots, and multiplicative scale factors, then clamp those factors and accept or reject steps. These formulations assume floating-point or real arithmetic. In Q15 with floor rounding, every coefficient product, error normalization, controller exponentiation or approximation, and step-size update therefore needs a specified integer realization. CMSIS-DSP supplies Q15 multiplication, shifts, conversion, accumulation, and explicit saturation facilities, but does not itself provide a fixed-point adaptive RK controller; overflow policy, intermediate width, quantized step grid, minimum nonzero error, and deterministic rejection limits remain implementation choices.

  • Bogacki, Shampine advances at order 3 and estimates error at order 2.
  • Dormand, Prince advances at order 5 and controls error with an embedded order-4 estimate.
  • Embedded pairs obtain an error estimate from two weighted combinations of shared stages.
  • PI controllers incorporate both present and previous error measurements.
  • Digital-filter analysis targets smoother step sequences and improved computational stability.
  • Practical controllers clamp step-growth and step-reduction factors.
  • Reference DOPRI5 code includes safety, minimum-factor, maximum-factor, and PI beta parameters.
  • Q15 controller realization requires explicit scaling, overflow, saturation, and rounding rules.

Rounding modes in fixed-point ODE solvers collected 2026-09-24 13:03 CT, epoch 2, cycle 984

Fixed-point ODE experiments show that rounding policy can materially change long-run trajectories. In tests involving four explicit solvers and an Izhikevich neuron model, stochastic rounding of multiplication results produced smaller spike-timing errors than round-to-nearest or discarded-bit rounding. Six random bits were sufficient in those experiments, while 16-bit arithmetic still suffered from overflow and underflow. The authors also measured about 30% overhead when replacing round-to-nearest with software stochastic rounding in an RK2 midpoint implementation; execution speed was governed largely by the pseudorandom-number generator.

These findings bear directly on Q15 integration with floor rounding. Floor is directionally biased, unlike stochastic rounding, whose expected rounded value equals the unquantized value. It is also distinct from simple signed truncation: an arithmetic right shift of a negative two's-complement product normally behaves like floor, whereas a conversion that truncates toward zero does not. CMSIS-DSP documents saturating Q15 operations and wider intermediate products followed by shifts, so the exact locations of narrowing shifts and saturation are part of the numerical method, not merely implementation details. Retaining wide products or accumulators until a stage sum is complete reduces the number of quantization events, while stochastic rounding introduces PRNG, masking, shifting, and addition work that may conflict with a fixed Cortex-M0+ cycle budget.

  • Directed floor rounding introduces a negative one-sided quantization error whenever a result is not exactly representable.
  • For negative values, arithmetic right shift implements floor-like behavior rather than truncation toward zero.
  • Stochastic rounding is unbiased in expectation and can prevent small updates from being lost systematically.
  • Fixed-point ODE tests found stochastic rounding more accurate than round-to-nearest and discarded-bit rounding across several explicit solvers.
  • Six random residual bits were sufficient in the cited neuron-model experiments, but this is empirical rather than universal.
  • The cited RK2 benchmark measured roughly 30% software overhead for stochastic rounding relative to round-to-nearest.
  • Wide intermediate products and deferred narrowing reduce how often rounding error enters an RK step.
  • Q15 saturation creates nonlinear error distinct from rounding error, so overflow counts and rounding bias should be evaluated separately.

Production ODE integrator architecture collected 2026-09-24 12:07 CT, epoch 2, cycle 960

SciPy’s solve_ivp defaults to the explicit Dormand, Prince RK45 pair with rtol=1e-3 and atol=1e-6. Its implementation stores stage derivatives in a NumPy array K of shape (stages+1, state dimension), forms the embedded error by a weighted combination of those rows, and evaluates an RMS norm after componentwise scaling by atol + rtol·max(|y_old|,|y_new|). The proportional controller uses safety 0.9, limits each change to [0.2,10], and prevents immediate growth after a rejected step. Thus its acceptance test and controller entail reductions, division, powers, and retry-dependent execution rather than a fixed cycle count.

Boost.Odeint separates the numerical algorithm from state storage through templated state, algebra, and operation types; supported states include std::array and std::vector, and custom fixed-size types can supply their own algebra. Its default RK checker scales each component against eps_abs + eps_rel·(a_x|x| + a_dxdt|dt·dxdt|), with a_x=a_dxdt=1 and default checker tolerances of 1e-6, then uses the algebra’s infinity-norm reduction. This design is more amenable to compile-time fixed storage than SciPy’s, although the controlled stepper still accepts, rejects, and resizes steps dynamically.

CVODE is not a Runge, Kutta implementation: it uses variable-step, variable-order Adams methods through order 12 or BDF through order 5. It operates through the abstract N_Vector interface; the serial implementation contains a length and pointer to a contiguous sunrealtype array, while custom vector backends are supported. Error-like quantities use a weighted RMS norm with weights 1/(rtol·|y_i|+atol_i). Defaults include automatic initial-step estimation, no positive minimum step, maximum step infinity, up to 500 internal steps per requested output, and Newton nonlinear iteration by default. Across all three libraries, conventional production control depends on scaled vector reductions and variable work, so a Q15 implementation with floor rounding and a fixed cycle budget would need explicitly bounded storage, arithmetic, reductions, and retry policy rather than directly inheriting these controllers.

  • SciPy uses an RMS norm of the embedded error divided by atol + rtol·max(|y_old|,|y_new|).
  • SciPy RK control uses safety 0.9 with step-change bounds 0.2 and 10.
  • Boost.Odeint decouples steppers from storage through state, algebra, and operation templates.
  • Boost’s default controlled-RK criterion includes both state magnitude and dt times derivative magnitude.
  • Boost controlled steppers reduce the scaled component errors with an infinity norm.
  • CVODE uses variable-order Adams or BDF multistep formulas, not Runge, Kutta formulas.
  • CVODE uses WRMS error tests with component weights 1/(rtol·|y_i|+atol_i).

RK stability under fixed cycle budgets collected 2026-09-24 11:17 CT, epoch 2, cycle 936

For the scalar test equation y′=λy, an explicit s-stage Runge, Kutta method has a polynomial stability function, so its stable step is bounded by the portion of the spectrum mapped into |R(hλ)|≤1. Classical low-stage methods provide only short negative-real-axis intervals: approximately 2 for Euler and second-order RK, 2.51 for third-order RK, and 2.79 for classical RK4. Stabilized explicit methods instead spend additional stages enlarging selected parts of the stability region; Chebyshev constructions can make the negative-real interval grow proportionally to s², although damping and internal amplification become important. Optimization for hyperbolic spectra similarly shows that merely adding stages need not improve throughput when both stable step and execution cost grow linearly with stage count.

Under a fixed cycle budget, an implicit method pays only when the explicit method's stability-imposed substeps cost more than the nonlinear or linear solves needed by the implicit step, while the implicit step remains accurate enough to exploit its larger stability region. A-stability alone is insufficient for strongly damped modes: L-stable methods such as Radau IIA are designed to suppress them, whereas stiff problems can also cause order reduction tied to stage order. These comparisons assume conventional arithmetic. Low-precision research reports that naïvely reducing precision in stabilized RK stages can reduce convergence order and cap attainable accuracy, while many-stage work explicitly controls internal roundoff amplification; therefore Q15 floor arithmetic requires implementation-specific stability and error measurements rather than relying solely on real-arithmetic regions.

  • Classical RK4 extends the negative-real stability interval only modestly relative to its four-stage cost.
  • Chebyshev-stabilized explicit RK can obtain negative-real stability intervals proportional to the square of the stage count.
  • Extra stages are beneficial only when stable-step growth exceeds their cycle cost for the relevant spectrum.
  • Implicit break-even depends on solve cost, explicit stability substepping, and the accuracy-limited step size.
  • L-stability matters when very stiff modes must be strongly damped.
  • Stiff order reduction can limit the practical benefit of an otherwise stable implicit RK method.
  • Naïve low-precision stages can lower convergence order and impose an accuracy floor.

Long-run finite-precision ODE error collected 2026-09-24 10:25 CT, epoch 2, cycle 912

Probabilistic analyses of deterministic ODE solvers model accumulated roundoff as random perturbations. Mosbach and Turner derive trajectory distributions depending on time, step size, and arithmetic precision, and demonstrate the effect for Euler, RK4, VODE, and RADAU5. The literature also reports the familiar error crossover: decreasing the step initially reduces truncation error, but eventually the larger operation count makes accumulated roundoff dominant. Thus, at a fixed final time, increasing the step count need not improve the computed solution indefinitely.

Modern backward-error results explain why unbiased stochastic rounding can accumulate more slowly than worst-case deterministic bounds suggest. Connolly, Higham, and Mary prove zero mean and mean independence for stochastic-rounding errors and obtain earliest-order probabilistic bounds proportional to the square root of the operation count rather than the operation count. These conclusions do not transfer directly to Q15 floor rounding: floor quantization is directionally biased, fixed-point error is absolute rather than relative, and repeated sub-LSB increments can be systematically lost. Consequently, random-walk error models can be useful comparison cases, but a floor-rounded implementation also requires a deterministic bias term whose propagation depends on the ODE dynamics and the exact ordering of each Runge, Kutta stage.

  • Truncation error falls with step size while accumulated arithmetic error generally grows with step count.
  • The resulting total-error curve can have a minimum at a nonzero step size.
  • Probabilistic ODE analysis predicts distributions of long-time trajectories rather than only worst-case bounds.
  • Square-root operation-count bounds require zero-mean or comparable statistical assumptions.
  • Stochastic rounding has a proved zero-mean property and can avoid small-increment stagnation.
  • Floor rounding is biased, so unbiased random-walk models are not directly valid for Q15 execution.
  • Fixed-point quantization supplies an absolute error scale set by the LSB.
  • Arithmetic ordering can materially change finite-precision Runge, Kutta error.
