How to read this

Each row pairs a machine-checkable predicate over per-cell statistics, for example slow.p3s4.heldout < slow.p4s4.heldout, with the mechanism and control the model recorded before the data could answer. p and s name an (order, stages) cell, the leading word picks the cost model, and heldout in a predicate is that cell elite's error on the held-out set, which selects elites and so is not a clean test set.

Verdicts come from code, never from the model. Once every cell a predicate names holds min_samples records, the predicate is evaluated against those cells' running statistics. d is Cohen's d; below 0.2 the verdict is inconclusive whichever way the comparison went, so a weak effect cannot be claimed. A predicate that names an empty cell is inconclusive too, because absence of evidence never refutes. n is the smallest record count among the named cells, and c is the cycle the verdict landed on (c123+ means proposed at cycle 123 and still open).

×N means the model posed that predicate N times over the run. The row shows the newest verdict and lists the id of every posing; mixed beside the verdict means those posings did not all get the same one.

Numerical search over Runge, Kutta tableaus collected 2026-09-23 23:46 CT, epoch 2, cycle 600

Runge, Kutta construction is commonly formulated as a constrained search: the Butcher coefficients must satisfy rooted-tree order conditions, while remaining degrees of freedom are optimized for quantities such as the norm of the principal truncation-error coefficients, the SSP coefficient, storage form, or a prescribed stability polynomial. RK-Opt implements searches over full tableaus with these objectives and constraints, while NodePy can evaluate order-condition residuals and principal-error norms using either floating-point or exact arithmetic. Unstructured numerical search has also been used to discover coefficients without earliest imposing a hand-derived parameterization of the solution family.

Stability-polynomial optimization can sometimes be separated from tableau realization. Ketcheson and Ahmadia formulate maximum-stable-step selection as least-deviation feasibility problems and solve them using convex subproblems and bisection; a compatible tableau must then be found separately. For fixed-point use, classical order and stability objectives are incomplete: internal perturbations are propagated according to implementation-dependent internal-stability polynomials, and algebraically equivalent implementations can have different amplification. Thus quantized coefficients, floor-rounded stage operations, accumulator scaling, and the exact evaluation form must be assessed alongside classical order residuals and error constants.

  • Order conditions define nonlinear equality constraints on the Butcher coefficients.
  • Extra stages or parameterized families leave degrees of freedom for secondary optimization.
  • Principal-error norms measure the leading local truncation-error coefficients, not fixed-point rounding error.
  • RK-Opt supports objectives including principal error and SSP coefficient, plus low-storage and stability-polynomial constraints.
  • NodePy provides exact or tolerance-based order checks and evaluates order-condition residuals.
  • Stability-polynomial optimization and full-tableau realization are distinct problems.
  • Unstructured numerical search can explore tableaus without an analytic family parameterization.
  • Internal error amplification depends on the chosen stage implementation, not only on the mathematical tableau.

Floor-rounding bias and stochastic alternatives collected 2026-09-23 22:58 CT, epoch 2, cycle 576

Fixed-point multiplication normally produces a wider intermediate that must be shifted back to the target format. Hopkins et al. identify a two’s-complement right shift without correction as round-down: unlike truncation toward zero, it maps to floor for both signs. Its error is therefore one-sided. MathWorks’ quantization analysis gives floor rounding an approximate mean error of −Δ/2 and variance Δ²/12, where Δ is one LSB. In a Q15 Runge, Kutta implementation, repeatedly quantizing coefficients, stage products, or state increments this way can create deterministic drift rather than cancellation. The same ODE study also reports that errors in truncated fixed-point constants can accumulate across integration steps.

  • Two’s-complement arithmetic right shift implements floor, not truncation toward zero.
  • Idealized floor quantization has mean error −0.5 LSB and variance LSB²/12.
  • Stochastic rounding makes the expected rounded value equal to the unrounded value.
  • Fixed-point ODE experiments found stochastic rounding more accurate than round-to-nearest and round-down across several explicit solvers and neuron regimes.
  • Software stochastic rounding requires a PRNG plus masking, shifting, and addition, making it costly under a fixed cycle budget.
  • Dither-like randomized constants were investigated as a cheaper way to recover sub-LSB effects.
  • Runge, Kutta heat-equation analysis found round-to-nearest errors correlated across steps and eventual stagnation, while stochastic-rounding errors accumulated more slowly.
  • CMSIS-DSP Q15 shifts saturate outputs outside 0x8000, 0x7FFF; saturation is a separate nonlinear error from rounding bias.

Embedded RK pairs under Q15 arithmetic collected 2026-09-23 22:10 CT, epoch 2, cycle 552

Bogacki, Shampine 3(2) and Dormand, Prince 5(4) estimate local error by sharing stages between formulas of different orders. The original Dormand, Prince work emphasizes small principal truncation terms and extended absolute-stability regions. Bogacki, Shampine requires fewer stages, while Dormand, Prince provides higher formal order; their practical comparison under a fixed cycle budget therefore depends on accepted-step cost, rejected steps, and whether reusable stages remain valid after quantized state and step-size updates.

PI and PID step-size research treats the numerical method and controller as a feedback system and reports smoother, more robust step sequences than purely heuristic updates. This theory ordinarily assumes a sufficiently continuous error signal and step-size actuator. Q15 floor operations instead make both signals discrete, and saturation is an additional implementation behavior documented by Arm. Reduced-precision ODE experiments also show that rounding policy can materially affect solver error: stochastic rounding outperformed deterministic round-to-nearest in the cited neural-ODE study. The available sources do not establish equivalent benefits for deterministic floor rounding, so fixed-point evaluation must model every scaling, floor, saturation, acceptance comparison, and attainable step size explicitly.

  • Dormand, Prince 5(4) was designed for small fifth-order truncation terms and enlarged stability regions.
  • Bogacki, Shampine 3(2) trades lower formal order for a smaller stage count.
  • PI/PID controllers use current and previous error information to damp step-size oscillation.
  • Quantized error estimates and step sizes violate the effectively continuous actuator assumptions of standard controller analysis.
  • Arm Q15 operations may saturate, so overflow behavior must be distinguished from floor rounding.

Production ODE integrator architecture collected 2026-09-23 21:23 CT, epoch 2, cycle 528

SciPy solve_ivp represents the state as a one-dimensional NumPy array and stores explicit Runge, Kutta stage derivatives in a dense array shaped (stages + 1, state dimension). RK23 and RK45 use a scaled RMS error norm, with component scales atol + rtol·max(|y_old|, |y_new|). Their proportional controller uses a 0.9 safety factor, limits step-size factors to [0.2, 10], and forbids growth immediately after a rejected step. Defaults exposed by solve_ivp are RK45, rtol=1e-3, atol=1e-6, automatic initial-step selection, and no finite maximum step.

Boost.Odeint separates state storage from arithmetic through State, Algebra, Operations, and Resizer policies, allowing fixed arrays and custom numeric types without prescribing one memory container. Its default controlled-RK checker scales each component by eps_abs + eps_rel·(a_x|x| + a_dxdt|dt·dx/dt|), then applies an infinity norm; its default tolerances are 1e-6, a_x=a_dxdt=1. The step adjuster uses a 0.9 safety factor, limits rejection shrinkage to no worse than 0.2, grows only when normalized error is below 0.5, and caps growth at fivefold. CVODE is structurally different: it uses variable-step, variable-order Adams or BDF multistep formulas, stores history in a Nordsieck array of abstract N_Vector objects, and applies weighted RMS norms with weights 1/(rtol|y_i|+atol_i). These production designs show that data-layout abstraction and explicit workspace reuse transfer readily to fixed-point implementations, whereas their floating-point RMS calculations, exponentiation-based adaptive controllers, rejection loops, and variable cycle counts do not directly match a fixed-cycle Cortex-M0+ execution model.

  • SciPy RK stages are stored stage-major in a reusable (s+1, n) NumPy array.
  • SciPy RK error control uses a scaled RMS norm rather than a componentwise maximum.
  • SciPy RK controller constants are safety 0.9, minimum factor 0.2, and maximum factor 10.
  • SciPy solve_ivp defaults to RK45 with rtol 1e-3 and atol 1e-6.
  • Boost.Odeint decouples state containers from elementwise operations through compile-time policies.
  • Boost.Odeint's default controlled-RK error test is an infinity norm with derivative-dependent scaling.
  • Boost.Odeint defaults its error-checker tolerances to 1e-6 and controller safety factor to 0.9.
  • CVODE stores scaled solution history in a Nordsieck representation over abstract N_Vector storage.

Long-run quantization error in fixed-point Runge, Kutta solvers collected 2026-09-23 20:36 CT, epoch 2, cycle 504

Classical truncation error decreases as the step size is reduced, but the number of steps and arithmetic operations then increases, allowing quantization error to dominate. Probabilistic analyses model local rounding errors as random perturbations propagated by the numerical flow; Mosbach and Turner derived trajectory distributions depending on time, step size, and precision, including long-time divergence when the dynamics amplify perturbations. Within each Runge, Kutta step, the tableau alone does not determine roundoff behavior: Ketcheson, Lóczi, and Parsani showed that algebraically equivalent implementations can have very different internal error-amplification polynomials.

The strongest square-root accumulation and backward-error results require unbiased errors. Connolly, Higham, and Mary proved that stochastic-rounding errors are zero-mean and mean-independent, giving high-probability backward-error bounds proportional to square root of the operation count, to earliest order, for broad linear-algebra computations. Fixed-point ODE experiments by Hopkins and coauthors likewise found that stochastic rounding can prevent stagnation and outperform round-to-nearest at low precision. These conclusions do not transfer directly to Q15 floor rounding: floor is directed and generally biased, so persistent errors can accumulate coherently rather than as a zero-mean random walk. Consequently, large-step-count evaluation should separately measure systematic drift, update stagnation, and amplification of stage-level errors, while balancing these against the method's decreasing truncation error.

  • Smaller steps reduce truncation error but increase the number of quantized operations.
  • Long-run roundoff depends on dynamical amplification as well as operation count.
  • Runge, Kutta implementation form can change internal error amplification.
  • Square-root probabilistic bounds rely on zero-mean or mean-independent rounding errors.
  • Deterministic floor rounding violates the unbiased-error premise.
  • Directed quantization can produce systematic drift or lost updates instead of random-walk cancellation.
  • Fixed-point ODE experiments identify stochastic rounding as a remedy for stagnation, but it has implementation cost.

Constant-step structure-preserving integration collected 2026-09-23 19:53 CT, epoch 2, cycle 480

Recent work offers energy-conserving modifications of Runge, Kutta schemes that retain fixed time steps. Najafian and Vermeire’s relaxation-free RK framework modifies RK weights at each step, retains the base method’s formal order, and conserves energy in its test problems with little additional computation. The correction nevertheless requires inner products, a discriminant, division, and a square root; a real correction is guaranteed only for sufficiently small steps. Relaxation exponential RK methods likewise provide high-order energy conservation or dissipation under stated step-size restrictions, but target semilinear PDEs and require exponential-integrator machinery.

These results support investigating constant-step conservation corrections because fixed steps simplify timing, but they do not establish fixed cycle counts on microcontrollers: data-dependent correction arithmetic and exceptional cases still require bounded implementations. The cited experiments use conventional machine arithmetic and do not analyze Q15 saturation, floor rounding, or Cortex-M0+ execution. Earlier work on split symplectic integrators also shows that compensated accumulation can reduce long-run roundoff substantially at little added cost, although that evidence is floating-point and problem-specific rather than a guarantee for floor-rounded fixed point.

  • Relaxation-free RK retains a constant step size and the order of its base RK method.

Cost structure of implicit RK families on small MCUs collected 2026-09-23 19:05 CT, epoch 2, cycle 456

Fully implicit methods such as Radau IIA solve a coupled nonlinear system containing all stage values. A direct Newton step involves an sd-by-sd linear system for an s-stage method applied to a d-dimensional ODE. Simplified Newton can reuse one factorization per time step, but every retained Newton iteration still requires residual evaluations and a coupled solve. SDIRK and ESDIRK exploit a repeated diagonal coefficient so stages can be solved sequentially and the same d-by-d factorization can be reused. Their execution time nevertheless depends on the prescribed number of nonlinear and linear iterations unless both are statically bounded.

Rosenbrock methods replace each nonlinear stage solve with one linearized solve. When every stage uses the same diagonal coefficient, all stages share the matrix I-hγJ and can reuse its factorization. This gives a naturally fixed number of stage solves, although Jacobian construction, factorization, and solve accuracy remain significant costs. Published comparisons show that insufficiently accurate linear solves can destroy nominal order, while fixed truncated nonlinear iterations are common when exact Newton convergence is too expensive. On Cortex-M0+, Q15 coefficient products require careful implementation because MULS returns only the low 32 bits and may take either one or 32 cycles depending on the core configuration; the resulting shift or division, saturation, accumulation width, and floor-rounding behavior must therefore be included explicitly in any fixed-cycle cost model.

  • Radau IIA couples all stages into an sd-by-sd Newton system.
  • Simplified Newton permits one factorization per step but not one solve per step.
  • SDIRK and ESDIRK process stages sequentially and can reuse a d-by-d factorization.
  • Fixed Newton counts make runtime bounded but do not guarantee convergence or classical order.
  • Rosenbrock methods use one linear solve per stage instead of nonlinear stage iteration.
  • A common Rosenbrock diagonal coefficient permits factorization reuse across stages.
  • Loose linear-solver tolerances can reduce the observed order of Rosenbrock-Wanner methods.
  • Cortex-M0+ multiplication latency is implementation-dependent: one or 32 cycles.

Cortex-M0+ constant-multiply cost models collected 2026-09-23 18:14 CT, epoch 2, cycle 432

Arm’s Cortex-M0+ Technical Reference Manual specifies an implementation-configurable 32×32-bit MULS instruction that returns the low 32 bits. Depending on the selected multiplier implementation, MULS takes either 1 or 32 cycles; basic register shifts, additions, and subtractions are listed as one cycle under the manual’s zero-wait-state assumptions. Consequently, a fixed cycle budget cannot use a generic “Cortex-M0+ multiply cost”: it must identify the target silicon’s multiplier configuration and account separately for memory-system wait states. GCC reflects this distinction with cortex-m0plus and cortex-m0plus.small-multiply processor models, and its generated treatment of constant multiplication can vary with target and optimization settings.

Canonical signed-digit representation uses digits −1, 0, and 1 with no adjacent nonzero digits, minimizing the number of nonzero digits in the representation. A constant product can therefore be expressed as additions and subtractions of shifted operands; for example, runs of binary ones can be replaced by a difference of powers of two. This gives a useful initial instruction-count model for Q15 Runge, Kutta coefficients on a slow-multiplier implementation. Literature on multiplierless constant multiplication also shows that minimum CSD digit count does not necessarily minimize the complete computation: factored or graph-based shift-add forms and shared intermediate expressions can reduce operation count or dependency depth, especially when several tableau constants multiply related values. Exact Cortex-M0+ assessment therefore requires compiling or constructing each candidate sequence and counting its instructions and dependencies under the selected core and memory configuration.

  • Cortex-M0+ MULS latency is implementation-configurable: 1 or 32 cycles.
  • MULS computes a 32×32-bit product and retains the least-significant 32 bits.
  • Register add, subtract, and shift instructions are nominally one cycle with zero wait states.
  • The target MCU’s actual multiplier variant must be established before comparing MULS with shift-add sequences.
  • GCC exposes cortex-m0plus.small-multiply as a distinct CPU model.
  • CSD minimizes nonzero signed digits and forbids adjacent nonzero digits.
  • A CSD constant product maps directly to shifted operands combined by additions and subtractions.
  • Minimum CSD weight does not guarantee the fewest instructions or shortest dependency chain.

Numerical optimization of low-order Runge, Kutta tableaus collected 2026-09-23 17:27 CT, epoch 2, cycle 408

Classical order conditions constrain a Butcher tableau through polynomial equations associated with rooted trees, but often leave free coefficients. Those degrees of freedom can be selected using secondary objectives. Ralston analytically minimized specified truncation-error bounds for second-, third-, and fourth-order methods; the familiar two-stage, second-order Ralston scheme is one result. Modern software measures accuracy using the vector of leading rooted-tree error coefficients: NodePy exposes individual coefficients, principal-error norms, and exact or floating-point order checks.

RK-Opt turns tableau construction into a constrained numerical search over a prescribed stage count, order, and method class. It can minimize the principal error norm or maximize an SSP coefficient, optionally while fixing a previously optimized stability polynomial. Its authors emphasize that coefficient optimization is highly nonconvex, so multiple starting points may produce different feasible or locally optimal results. RootedTrees.jl supplies current symbolic infrastructure for generating order conditions. These sources optimize mathematical tableaus over real coefficients; they do not incorporate Q15 representability, floor-rounding bias, instruction count, or intermediate overflow into the stated objectives, so those properties require additional discrete constraints or evaluation after coefficient quantization.

  • Order conditions are polynomial equalities indexed by rooted trees.
  • Low-order explicit RK families retain free parameters after the target order is imposed.
  • Ralston selected free parameters by minimizing specified truncation-error bounds.
  • Principal error norm is the norm of the leading-order rooted-tree error coefficients.
  • RK-Opt searches by stage count, order, method class, bounds, and optional stability constraints.
  • RK-Opt supports accuracy and SSP objectives but documents a highly nonconvex search problem.
  • Optimal stability-polynomial search and realization as a full RK tableau can be separate steps.
  • Exact order verification is useful after replacing real coefficients with Q15-representable values.

Embedded RK pairs and quantized PI step control collected 2026-09-23 16:40 CT, epoch 2, cycle 384

Classical proportional step selection uses the current embedded-error estimate; the Gustafsson, Lundh, Söderlind PI approach also uses error history and was introduced to make adaptation more robust, particularly when stability limits the step size. In Q15, the controller cannot directly preserve the literature’s real-valued power laws: coefficient products require widened intermediates and explicit rescaling, while saturation and floor rounding can alter both the error estimate and the next-step decision. ARM’s CMSIS-DSP documentation confirms saturating Q15 multiplication but does not define a fixed-point embedded-RK or PI-controller implementation, so admissible step sizes, zero-error handling, rejection limits, scaling, and rounding semantics must be specified separately.
