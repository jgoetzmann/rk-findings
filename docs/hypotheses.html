How to read this

Verdicts come from code, never from the model. Once every cell a predicate names holds min_samples records, the predicate is evaluated against those cells' running statistics. d is Cohen's d; below 0.2 the verdict is inconclusive whichever way the comparison went, so a weak effect cannot be claimed. A predicate that names an empty cell is inconclusive too, because absence of evidence never refutes. n is the smallest record count among the named cells, and c is the cycle the verdict landed on (c123+ means proposed at cycle 123 and still open).

×N means the model posed that predicate N times over the run. The row shows the newest verdict and lists the id of every posing; mixed beside the verdict means those posings did not all get the same one.

Cost structure of implicit RK families on Cortex-M0+ collected 2026-09-23 00:08 CT, epoch 2, cycle 144

SDIRK methods solve stages sequentially. Their common diagonal coefficient gives each Newton linearization the same matrix form, so a factorization can be reused across stages when the Jacobian and step size are held fixed. Fully implicit Radau IIA instead couples all stage unknowns; straightforward simplified Newton therefore produces a larger block system. Transformations and splittings can reduce Radau's factorization cost, sometimes to one state-sized factorization, but retain stage transformations, block substitutions, and convergence-dependent iterations. Literature on inexact simplified Newton identifies the nonlinear and linear solves as the principal implementation bottleneck. Work on fixed finite Newton counts shows that specially constructed generalized RK schemes can preserve the parent formula's order and linear stability, but this is a designed property rather than a general guarantee obtained by truncating any implicit solver after an arbitrary number of iterations.

Rosenbrock methods remove nonlinear iteration by linearizing the stages in the method definition. Each stage requires a right-hand-side evaluation and a linear solve; with a common diagonal gamma, all stages can reuse one factorization of I-h*gamma*J. Rosenbrock-W methods additionally permit approximate Jacobians. This makes their operation schedule more deterministic than convergence-tested Newton implementations, although Jacobian formation, factorization, and triangular solves remain costs. On Cortex-M0+, Arm documents one-cycle integer adds and shifts, but MULS may take either 1 or 32 cycles depending on the configured multiplier; loads and stores also have implementation-dependent timing. Consequently, Q15 cycle estimates must target the actual core implementation and explicitly count coefficient products, scaling shifts, memory traffic, Jacobian work, and linear-solver arithmetic. The consulted numerical literature analyzes real or floating-point arithmetic and does not establish accuracy or stability guarantees for Q15 floor rounding, so those effects require separate finite-precision analysis.

  • SDIRK stages are sequential nonlinear solves, but a common diagonal can permit reuse of one linear-system factorization.
  • Radau IIA couples all stages and has a larger native Newton system.
  • Radau transformations and splittings trade extra stage algebra for fewer or smaller factorizations.
  • Rosenbrock schemes replace nonlinear stage iteration with prescribed linear solves.
  • A common Rosenbrock diagonal permits one factorization to serve every stage of a step.
  • Rosenbrock-W methods allow an approximate Jacobian, subject to their own order conditions.
  • Arbitrarily stopping Newton after a fixed count does not generally preserve the parent RK method's properties.
  • Fixed-iteration schemes with preserved order and stability exist when constructed and analyzed specifically for that iteration rule.

Production ODE integrator architecture collected 2026-09-17 16:35 CT, epoch 2, cycle 48

CVODE is not a Runge, Kutta implementation: it uses variable-order, variable-step Adams, Moulton formulas for nonstiff systems or BDF formulas for stiff systems. Its state interface is N_Vector; the serial implementation contains a length, ownership flag, and pointer to a contiguous scalar array. CVODE applies a weighted RMS norm with weights 1/(rtol·|y_i|+atol_i), retries failed steps at a smaller size, and jointly varies step size and method order. Documented controller constants include a 1/6 safety factor in the principal step-size estimate, maximum ordinary growth of 10, earliest-step growth up to 10^4, and suppression of changes below a ratio of 1.5. These libraries therefore supply three distinct production patterns relevant to a fixed-cycle, fixed-point search: dense stage-buffered embedded RK with RMS acceptance, compile-time-generic RK with a maximum-component test, and history-buffered multistep integration with weighted RMS control. Their adaptive controllers and floating-point tolerance formulas require additional policy decisions when cycles are fixed and Q15 floor rounding introduces a non-negligible error quantum.

Long-run roundoff, truncation balance in low-precision ODE solvers collected 2026-09-17 14:15 CT, epoch 2, cycle 24

Classical finite-precision analysis gives the global error of an order-p fixed-step ODE method as O(h^p + u/h): truncation error decreases with the step size h, while a worst-case roundoff contribution grows with the number of steps. Probabilistic models with independent, zero-mean local errors predict milder accumulation, commonly proportional to u/sqrt(h). Experiments and analysis for RK4 and other solvers show that unstable dynamics can amplify these perturbations and eventually produce distributions of trajectories rather than a single reliably convergent path. A posteriori analysis likewise finds accumulated roundoff inversely proportional to the square root of the step size and concludes that finite precision imposes an eventual accuracy limit.

  • Reducing the step size eventually increases total error once accumulated roundoff dominates truncation error.
  • The conventional worst-case balance is O(h^p + u/h) for an order-p method.
  • Zero-mean probabilistic models can reduce step-count growth from linear to approximately square-root behavior.
  • Floor rounding is directed and biased, so zero-mean cancellation assumptions used in probabilistic analyses do not apply directly.
  • In Q15, the representable spacing is 2^-15; unlike floating point, this is an absolute spacing across the usable range.
  • Fixed-point experiments found GCC round-down behavior and quantized constants detrimental to ODE accuracy.
  • Keeping products and intermediate values at higher precision before the final quantization reduces arithmetic error.
  • Long-time error depends on the ODE's perturbation amplification as well as on the RK order and local quantization size.

Embedded RK pairs and fixed-point step control collected 2026-09-08 21:39 CT, epoch 1, cycle 2608

Bogacki, Shampine introduced an embedded 3(2) Runge, Kutta pair whose shared stages provide a third-order update and a second-order error estimate. The commonly used scheme has four stages and the earliest-same-as-last property, allowing the final derivative of an accepted step to be reused as the earliest derivative of the next step. These results show that nominal stage count alone does not determine recurring cost: derivative reuse, accepted-step rate, coefficient arithmetic, and stability-limited step size also matter. Budgeted low-precision RK and energy behavior collected 2026-09-08 21:05 CT, epoch 1, cycle 2600

Recent mixed-precision Runge, Kutta research treats arithmetic precision and stage evaluation placement as separate design variables. A 2026 study of Bogacki, Shampine 3(2) found that mixed precision can retain much of the high-precision accuracy, but low precision did not reduce the number of function evaluations enough to offset slower operations in the tested setting. Work on reduced-precision fixed-point ODE solvers also shows that rounding mode materially affects trajectory error: stochastic rounding outperformed deterministic round-to-nearest in the reported neuron-model experiments. Because floor rounding is directional rather than unbiased, classical order and stage count alone do not characterize its accumulated error.

Structure preservation introduces another axis beyond classical order. Explicit pseudo-energy-preserving RK schemes have been constructed with energy-preservation order higher than their classical order; tests reported smaller errors than same-order RK methods over moderately long Hamiltonian simulations. Thus, comparisons among four-, five-, and six-stage archive cells require coefficient-level measures of quantization sensitivity and invariant drift, not only nominal order. Cortex-M0+ fixed-point multiplication costs collected 2026-09-08 20:35 CT, epoch 1, cycle 2592

Arm documents two implementation-time multiplier choices for Cortex-M0+: a fast single-cycle array multiplier and a smaller 32-cycle iterative multiplier. Both execute MULS, which returns the low 32 bits of a 32-by-32-bit product. The core timing table lists register addition, subtraction, and immediate or register shifts as one-cycle instructions, while loads can take one or two cycles depending on the implementation. Consequently, a cost model that assigns one universal price to multiplication does not represent all Cortex-M0+ devices. For signed Q15 operands, the full product fits within 31 significant bits, so MULS followed by ASRS #15 can implement the usual arithmetic-shift floor scaling without needing a 64-bit product.

Constant multiplication can instead be expressed as shifts plus additions or subtractions. Canonical signed-digit encoding minimizes the number of nonzero signed digits, but it does not necessarily produce the shortest possible addition chain; adder-graph methods can reuse intermediate expressions and sometimes improve on direct CSD expansion. GCC explicitly distinguishes Cortex-M0/M0+/M1 targets with the small iterative multiplier and may replace multiplication with add/subtract/shift sequences. These findings support treating fast-multiplier, slow-multiplier, and constant-specific shift-add costs separately when comparing Runge-Kutta cells: stage count alone does not determine cycles, because coefficient encodings, reused partial products, constant materialization, scaling shifts, and register spills can materially change the arithmetic cost.

  • Cortex-M0+ offers either a one-cycle or a 32-cycle MULS implementation.
  • MULS returns the least-significant 32 bits of a 32-by-32-bit product.
  • Basic register adds, subtracts, and shifts are listed as one-cycle operations.
  • ASRS supplies sign-preserving right shifts suitable for floor-style power-of-two scaling.
  • A signed Q15-by-Q15 product fits in the available 32-bit MULS result.
  • CSD reduces nonzero signed digits but is not always the optimal addition chain.
  • Adder graphs can share partial products across several constant multiplications.
  • GCC has distinct small-multiplier Cortex-M0+ tuning that favors shift-add replacements.

Numerical optimization of Runge, Kutta tableaus collected 2026-09-08 20:06 CT, epoch 1, cycle 2584

Runge, Kutta design is naturally a constrained optimization problem: the Butcher coefficients must satisfy polynomial order conditions, while remaining degrees of freedom can optimize leading truncation-error norm, stability, SSP behavior, or storage. RK-Opt supports searches indexed by stage count and order, including minimization of the principal error norm. Its documentation stresses that coefficient optimization is highly nonconvex, so multistart searches can converge to different local solutions and do not automatically certify global optimality. Classical constructions likewise use free parameters and numerical searches to reduce error coefficients; Dormand and Prince’s search over a parameterized family is a documented example.

Extra stages can therefore improve a method even without raising its formal order, but neither order nor stage count alone determines realized error. Higher order suppresses truncation error asymptotically, whereas principal-error optimization addresses only the leading exact-arithmetic term. Internal-error research shows that roundoff or inexact stage computations can be amplified and that amplification depends strongly on the implementation. Consequently, the literature makes an order-4 five-stage advantage over order-2 or order-3 cells plausible in a truncation-dominated regime, but it does not establish H-682 or H-684 through H-687 under Q15 floor rounding and fixed cycle budgets; those comparisons require objectives or validation that explicitly include quantized coefficients, floor-rounded stage arithmetic, implementation form, and the applicable cost model.

  • Order conditions constrain tableau coefficients through nonlinear polynomial equalities.
  • Free parameters left after satisfying order conditions can be optimized for principal error, stability, SSP properties, or storage.
  • Adding stages can create useful design freedom without increasing formal order.
  • Principal error norm measures leading truncation terms, not total fixed-point execution error.
  • RK coefficient searches are highly nonconvex and commonly use many random or multistart solves.
  • Small error constants in exact arithmetic do not guarantee small error under Q15 floor rounding.
  • Internal perturbation amplification depends on the particular RK implementation, not only its Butcher tableau.
  • The cited theory does not by itself rank the specified archive cells under slow, AVR-approximate, or fast cycle models.

RK stability under fixed cycle budgets collected 2026-09-08 19:36 CT, epoch 1, cycle 2576

For an explicit Runge, Kutta method, linear stability is governed by a polynomial whose earliest coefficients are fixed by the requested order and whose remaining coefficients can enlarge the stability region. Recent work formulates method selection as maximizing the admissible step for the actual eigenvalue spectrum. Consequently, stage count and order alone do not establish superiority: a five-stage fourth-order method has fewer free stability-polynomial coefficients than a six-stage second-order method, while a four-stage third-order method can devote its extra coefficient to stability. Runge, Kutta, Chebyshev results show the limiting alternative for diffusion-like spectra: low-order stabilized explicit methods can obtain a negative-real stability interval proportional to the square of the stage count, although their applicability assumes spectra concentrated near the negative real axis. Internal amplification of roundoff is a separate constraint and becomes important in low precision.

Under a fixed cycle budget, the relevant comparison is stable simulated time and achieved error per cycle, not error per step. Extra stages pay when their larger stable step or higher-order accuracy compensates for fewer completed steps; optimized stability must also match whether the problem spectrum is real-negative or substantially complex. Implicit DIRK and ESDIRK methods provide stiffly accurate, L-stable formulas, but each implicit stage requires solving an algebraic system. They pay for themselves only when explicit stability restrictions would otherwise require enough additional steps to exceed those solve costs. That crossover moves against implicit methods on Cortex-M0+, where hardware division is absent and multiplication latency is implementation-dependent; fixed-point nonlinear solves also require convergence tests, scaling, and safeguards. For the stated comparisons, the literature therefore supports measuring spectrum-specific stability extent, internal roundoff amplification under Q15 floor rounding, and total cycles per accepted unit of simulated time alongside held-out error.

  • Explicit RK stability is determined by a degree-s stability polynomial constrained by order conditions.
  • At fixed stage count, higher order leaves fewer coefficients available for stability optimization.
  • A five-stage fourth-order stability polynomial has one coefficient beyond the required exponential terms.
  • A four-stage third-order stability polynomial likewise has one free stability coefficient.
  • A six-stage second-order polynomial has four coefficients beyond its order constraints.
  • RKC negative-real stability extent grows proportionally to the square of the stage count.
  • RKC theory targets spectra lying in a narrow strip around the negative real axis.
  • Stability-region area is less informative than containment of the scaled problem spectrum.

Quantized Runge, Kutta rounding and MCU fixed point collected 2026-09-08 19:06 CT, epoch 1, cycle 2568

Runge, Kutta error in low precision contains both discretization error and propagated arithmetic perturbations. General RK analysis shows that roundoff introduced at successive steps can accumulate globally, while reduced-precision analyses give a deterministic roundoff contribution proportional to u/h in addition to the method term h^p. Consequently, decreasing the step size or increasing formal order does not necessarily reduce total error once Q15 quantization dominates. Floor rounding is directed and introduces a non-positive error at every nonexact quantization, so its errors cannot be modeled as unbiased independent noise; repeated stage multiplications and state updates can create systematic drift.

Experiments with fixed-point neural ODE solvers found stochastic rounding more accurate than round-to-nearest fixed point and even binary32 for the studied neuron models and solvers. That work used a fixed-step, second-order Runge, Kutta variant optimized for fixed-point execution and reported that a small random residual was sufficient for stochastic rounding. Related Runge, Kutta heat-equation analysis found that implementation details affect error accumulation and that stochastic rounding can avoid stagnation, although random-number generation adds work and conflicts with a tightly deterministic cycle budget.

For Cortex-M implementations, Q15 stores 15 fractional bits in a signed 16-bit value and Q31 uses a signed 32-bit representation. Arm CMSIS-DSP supplies wider intermediate storage and both saturating and nonsaturating operations, making accumulator width, rescaling points, and saturation behavior part of the numerical method. A five- or six-stage RK cell has more potential quantization sites than a two-stage cell, but the resulting error depends on where values are narrowed and whether products are accumulated at wider precision. Thus formal order and stage count alone do not determine the comparisons in H-668, H-669, H-671, H-672, or H-673; they require evaluation with the exact floor-rounding schedule, scaling, overflow policy, and target instruction costs.

  • Deterministic low-precision RK error can contain an O(u/h) global component alongside O(h^p) discretization error.
  • Floor rounding is one-sided and can produce systematic drift rather than zero-mean roundoff.
  • More RK stages create more possible rounding and narrowing sites unless intermediates remain wide.
  • Stochastic rounding reduces bias and stagnation in published low-precision ODE experiments.
  • Random-number generation makes stochastic rounding unsuitable as a cost-free substitution under a fixed cycle budget.
  • Q15 spans -1 through 1-2^-15; scaling is therefore necessary for states, derivatives, or coefficients outside that interval.
  • Wider products and accumulators can materially change accuracy without changing the nominal Q15 state format.
  • Saturation, overflow, coefficient quantization, and the exact placement of right shifts must be included in method comparisons.

Production ODE integrator architecture collected 2026-09-08 18:37 CT, epoch 1, cycle 2560

SciPy’s default solve_ivp method, RK45, stores the state as a one-dimensional NumPy array and the stage derivatives in a contiguous matrix shaped (stages + 1, state dimension). Its embedded error vector is divided componentwise by atol + rtol·max(|y_old|, |y_new|), then reduced with an RMS norm. Defaults are rtol=1e-3 and atol=1e-6. The proportional step controller uses safety 0.9, permits factors from 0.2 to 10, and prevents immediate growth following a rejected step. Boost.Odeint instead templates its steppers over the state container, algebra, operations, resizer, error checker, and step adjuster. Its default checker scales each component by eps_abs + eps_rel·(a_x|x| + a_dxdt|dt·dx/dt|), with eps_abs=eps_rel=1e-6 and both scale coefficients equal to 1; controlled Runge, Kutta steppers own reusable derivative, output, and error buffers and reduce the scaled error through the selected algebra.

CVODE is not a Runge, Kutta implementation: it uses variable-order Adams, Moulton formulas for nonstiff systems or BDF formulas for stiff systems, together with solution-history vectors, predictor-corrector data, and abstract N_Vector storage supporting multiple backends. It applies a weighted RMS norm with weights 1/(rtol·|y_i|+atol_i). Its controller jointly selects step size and order, begins at order 1, uses a 1/6 safety factor in the local-error step calculation, normally caps growth at 10, and retries rejected steps with additional failure-dependent limits. These libraries therefore demonstrate that production adaptive solvers incur costs beyond tableau stages: full-vector stage or history storage, component scaling, squared-error accumulation, square roots, controller powers, rejection paths, and sometimes nonlinear solves. Their designs do not establish any of H-662 through H-666, which compare fixed-cycle, fixed-point archive cells; they instead identify operations that should be excluded, fixed, or explicitly charged when relating floating-point library behavior to a Q15 Cortex-M0+ cost model.

  • SciPy RK45 stores stage derivatives in a (stages + 1) by state-dimension NumPy array.
  • SciPy uses an RMS norm of the componentwise scaled embedded error.
  • SciPy RK defaults are rtol=1e-3, atol=1e-6, safety=0.9, minimum factor=0.2, and maximum factor=10.
  • Boost.Odeint abstracts state layout through container, algebra, operations, and resizer template parameters.
  • Boost’s default error scale includes both state magnitude and dt times derivative magnitude.
  • Boost default_error_checker defaults eps_abs and eps_rel to 1e-6 and both scale coefficients to 1.
  • CVODE uses Adams, Moulton or BDF multistep formulas, not Runge, Kutta tableaux.
  • CVODE represents vectors through the N_Vector abstraction and retains multistep history.
