How to read this

Each row pairs a machine-checkable predicate over per-cell statistics, for example slow.p3s4.heldout < slow.p4s4.heldout, with the mechanism and control the model recorded before the data could answer. p and s name an (order, stages) cell, the leading word picks the cost model, and heldout in a predicate is that cell elite's error on the held-out set, which selects elites and so is not a clean test set.

Verdicts come from code, never from the model. Once every cell a predicate names holds min_samples records, the predicate is evaluated against those cells' running statistics. d is Cohen's d; below 0.2 the verdict is inconclusive whichever way the comparison went, so a weak effect cannot be claimed. A predicate that names an empty cell is inconclusive too, because absence of evidence never refutes. n is the smallest record count among the named cells, and c is the cycle the verdict landed on (c123+ means proposed at cycle 123 and still open).

×N means the model posed that predicate N times over the run. The row shows the newest verdict and lists the id of every posing; mixed beside the verdict means those posings did not all get the same one.

Long-run roundoff and ODE error models collected 2026-09-25 03:49 CT, epoch 2, cycle 1350

At a fixed integration horizon, reducing the step size lowers truncation error but increases the number of rounded updates. Kehlet and Logg derive an accumulated roundoff term proportional to the inverse square root of step size under their error model. Mosbach and Turner analyze how rounding errors can produce a distribution of trajectories, especially in systems that amplify small perturbations. Both results show why more steps need not improve the final answer.

The rounding model matters for Q15 arithmetic. Hopkins and colleagues identify right-shift truncation of two’s-complement fixed-point values as round-down and show that rounding choice changes ODE accuracy in their neural-model experiments. Its directed error does not meet a zero-mean assumption without further evidence. Backward error analysis interprets a numerical integrator through a nearby modified ODE and helps explain long-time discretization behavior; floor-rounded arithmetic adds perturbations that must also be accounted for.

  • Smaller steps trade lower truncation error for more rounded operations.
  • Square-root accumulation depends on the rounding-error model.
  • Two’s-complement right-shift truncation rounds down.
  • Directed floor error should not be treated as zero-mean noise.
  • Modified-equation analysis addresses long-time integrator behavior.

Cortex-M0+ constant multiplication collected 2026-09-25 01:24 CT, epoch 2, cycle 1275

Arm documents two Cortex-M0+ multiplier implementations: MULS takes either 1 or 32 cycles and returns only the low 32 bits of a 32×32 product. Shifts, adds, and subtracts each take one cycle in Arm’s zero-wait-state timing table. The multiplier choice is specific to the MCU: Microchip lists a single-cycle multiplier for SAM C20/C21, and Raspberry Pi lists one for RP2040.

  • Check the target MCU’s multiplier configuration before assigning a cycle cost to MULS.
  • MULS does not produce the high half of a 32×32 product.
  • CSD reduces the number of nonzero signed digits; its minimum digit count does not by itself establish the fastest instruction sequence.
  • For floor-rounded Q15 arithmetic, keep the shift-add numerator exact through accumulation and apply the final right shift once; shifting terms separately can change the result.
  • Arm’s published instruction cycles assume zero wait states, so a fixed budget still needs timing on the target memory system.

Optimizing low-order Runge, Kutta tableaus collected 2026-09-24 22:39 CT, epoch 2, cycle 1200

Runge, Kutta order conditions are polynomial equations in the tableau coefficients. They can leave free parameters after the desired order is satisfied, allowing a search for smaller leading truncation-error terms or different stability properties. A two-stage, second-order example is Ralston’s method: its free stage location is chosen to minimize a local truncation-error bound. Numerical searches can also work directly with order conditions; RK-Opt provides tools for coefficient optimization at a specified order and stage count.

  • Treat order conditions as constraints and remaining tableau coefficients as search variables.
  • The objective matters: a small leading error constant does not by itself establish the best stability or implementation cost.
  • For Q15 floor arithmetic, evaluate quantized coefficients and stage computations separately from the real-valued tableau.
  • Internal error propagation depends on how a Runge, Kutta method is implemented.

Quantized Runge, Kutta arithmetic collected 2026-09-24 21:56 CT, epoch 2, cycle 1200

Finite-precision perturbations introduced at successive Runge, Kutta steps propagate through the method, and decreasing the step size does not necessarily reduce total error once roundoff dominates. A low-precision Runge, Kutta study of the heat equation found that round-to-nearest errors can grow like O(u/Δt) before the numerical solution stagnates, whereas stochastic rounding makes leading errors zero-mean and substantially reduces their accumulation. It also found that a delta-form update, which computes and then adds the state increment, is important for limiting rounding error.

Floor rounding differs from the unbiased model assumed for stochastic rounding: each representable result is no greater than the exact result, so its local quantization error is one-sided. Repeated stage multiplications and state updates can therefore produce systematic drift rather than cancellation, although the ODE dynamics determine how those perturbations propagate. For Q15/Q31 implementations, Arm documents saturating fixed-point multiplication and wider intermediate formats; its Q15 dot product retains 2.30 products in a 64-bit 34.30 accumulator, while its Q31 dot product discards 14 low bits before accumulation. Thus, accumulator width, the exact locations of right shifts or requantization, saturation, and coefficient scaling are all part of the numerical method and must be modeled explicitly under a fixed cycle budget.

  • Roundoff can dominate truncation error as the timestep decreases.
  • Deterministic rounding errors may correlate across timesteps and accumulate coherently.
  • Floor rounding has one-sided local error and is not an unbiased quantizer.
  • Stochastic rounding removes leading-order bias but requires random-bit generation and extra work.
  • Delta-form state updates can reduce loss of small increments.
  • Q15 multiplication naturally produces a 2.30 intermediate result.
  • Wide accumulation can postpone requantization and reduce repeated precision loss.
  • Saturation and discarded low bits must be included in bit-accurate RK analysis.

Embedded RK pairs and fixed-point step control collected 2026-09-24 19:46 CT, epoch 2, cycle 1176

Bogacki, Shampine 3(2) and Dormand, Prince 5(4) estimate local error by combining a shared set of Runge, Kutta stages with two different output formulas. Both support earliest-same-as-last stage reuse: after an accepted step, Bogacki, Shampine needs approximately three new derivative evaluations, while Dormand, Prince needs six. The original Dormand, Prince work selected fifth-order formulas for small principal truncation terms or enlarged absolute-stability regions, so stage count alone does not describe the accuracy-versus-cost tradeoff.

Step-size selection is itself a feedback system. Söderlind’s control formulation includes PI controllers and digital filters, while later stability analysis shows that a conventional one-error I controller can be unstable for some method/problem combinations and that PI or PID parameters can instead be designed for stable control dynamics. On Q15 hardware, this controller must operate within the representable range and explicit saturation/conversion rules documented by CMSIS-DSP. The sources consulted do not establish that floating-point controller gains retain their stability properties after Q15 coefficient quantization, floor rounding, discrete step-size choices, or deadline-driven rejection limits; those implementation effects therefore require integer-level analysis or testing. A recent resource-constrained implementation preprint uses bounded step changes and hysteresis to limit timing variability, illustrating one practical approach, but its experiments concern an STM32F407 and FPGA rather than Cortex-M0+.

  • Bogacki, Shampine is an embedded 3(2), four-stage, FSAL pair.
  • Dormand, Prince 5(4) has seven stages and reuses the last stage after accepted steps.
  • Embedded estimates reuse stage derivatives rather than running two independent integrators.
  • PI control uses present and previous error information to damp step-size oscillations.
  • Controller stability depends jointly on the Runge, Kutta method, estimator, and controller parameters.
  • Q15 implementations need explicit policies for scaling, saturation, multiplication, and format conversion.
  • The consulted literature does not validate PI-controller behavior under Q15 floor rounding.
  • Bounded step ratios and hysteresis can constrain adaptive-control timing variability.

Production ODE solver implementation patterns collected 2026-09-24 19:00 CT, epoch 2, cycle 1152

SciPy's explicit Runge, Kutta implementation stores the state as an n-element NumPy array and stage derivatives in a row-major logical array K of shape (stages + 1, n). It scales each embedded error component by atol + rtol * max(abs(y_old), abs(y_new)), then applies a root-mean-square norm. The default tolerances are rtol=1e-3 and atol=1e-6. Its proportional controller uses a 0.9 safety factor, limits a single change to factors between 0.2 and 10, and prevents immediate growth following a rejected attempt. Boost.Odeint instead abstracts state storage and arithmetic through State, Algebra, and Operations templates; ordinary arrays and vectors use elementwise range algebra, while controlled steppers reduce the scaled error with an infinity norm. Its default error checker uses absolute and relative tolerances of 1e-6, and its default controller uses a 0.9 multiplier, a maximum fivefold increase, and a maximum fivefold decrease per attempt.

  • SciPy RK stages are stored as K[stage, component], requiring stages + 1 full derivative vectors.
  • SciPy uses a weighted RMS error norm and accepts when the norm is below 1.
  • SciPy's RK scale references the larger magnitude of the old and proposed states.

Long-run roundoff and truncation error in quantized ODE solvers collected 2026-09-24 18:06 CT, epoch 2, cycle 1128

ODE solvers commonly show a V-shaped error curve as the step size decreases: truncation error initially falls, but eventually quantization or roundoff dominates because more steps and arithmetic operations inject more numerical error. Fixed-point experiments confirm that implementation details, including intermediate-variable ordering and coefficient quantization, can materially change the result. Runge, Kutta stage residuals can also be amplified within a step, and this internal amplification depends on the particular algebraic implementation, not just the Butcher tableau.

Probabilistic analyses obtain square-root-of-step-count growth when per-step roundoff is approximately independent and zero-mean. Systematic errors instead accumulate linearly; published Runge, Kutta studies identify rounded coefficients and solver termination effects as sources of such drift. Floor rounding is intrinsically one-sided except at exactly representable values, so the zero-mean random-walk model does not apply without additional symmetry or randomization. Fixed-point ODE experiments report accumulating errors from truncated constants and arithmetic, while stochastic rounding substantially reduces error in tested low-precision solvers. Backward-error analysis can explain bounded long-time errors when a numerical method preserves a nearby modified invariant, but coefficient quantization or biased arithmetic may destroy the structural conditions on which that conclusion depends.

  • Reducing the step size eventually increases roundoff exposure because the number of steps grows.
  • Zero-mean independent local errors predict root-N accumulation; biased errors can produce N-scale drift.
  • Floor rounding generally introduces a signed bias and therefore conflicts with the usual Brouwer-law assumptions.
  • Quantized Runge, Kutta coefficients can create systematic local error even when arithmetic noise is otherwise unbiased.
  • Internal stage-error amplification depends strongly on the implemented Runge, Kutta representation.
  • Fixed-point solver accuracy is sensitive to constant conversion, scaling, operation ordering, and intermediate precision.
  • Stochastic rounding removes deterministic directional bias in expectation and improved accuracy in published fixed-point ODE experiments.
  • Backward-error conclusions about long-time invariant behavior require preservation of the relevant discrete structure.
