Instruction-level cost of fixed-point constant products collected 2026-09-08 02:13 CT, cycle 2384

Arm’s Cortex-M0+ specification makes multiplication cost implementation-dependent: MULS computes the low 32 bits of a 32-by-32-bit product and takes either 1 cycle with the fast array multiplier or 32 cycles with the small iterative multiplier. Under the same zero-wait-state assumptions, register addition, subtraction, and immediate or register shifts take 1 cycle each. For a signed Q15 product whose full 16-by-16-bit result fits in 32 bits, an arithmetic right shift can implement the scale reduction and floor behavior for negative two’s-complement values without a separate division instruction. Loads, spills, branches, and coefficient materialization can still add costs beyond this arithmetic count.

CSD represents an integer constant with nonadjacent signed digits and minimizes its number of nonzero digits, reducing the straightforward number of shifted partial products. Constant-multiplication research nevertheless distinguishes sparse CSD encoding from globally minimal shift-add graphs: alternative signed-digit representations and shared intermediate expressions can require fewer additions across multiple constants. This means the relative costs of Runge, Kutta stage counts can change sharply between the fast- and slow-multiplier models, while an AVR-like shift-add model should count actual shifts, additions, register pressure, and reusable coefficient subexpressions rather than treating CSD digit count alone as the cycle cost.

  • Cortex-M0+ MULS is a silicon option costing either 1 or 32 cycles.
  • MULS returns the least-significant 32 bits of a 32-by-32-bit product.
  • Adds, subtracts, and arithmetic or logical shifts are each 1 cycle in the zero-wait-state core timing table.
  • Arithmetic right shift supplies floor-style power-of-two scaling for signed two’s-complement values.
  • CSD minimizes nonzero signed digits but does not guarantee the minimum shift-add graph.
  • Common subexpression sharing can reduce the cost of several constant products.
  • Hardware-oriented literature treats fixed shifts as wiring, but shifts consume instructions on Cortex-M0+.
  • Multiplier variant can reverse the tradeoff between extra stages and multiplierless coefficient evaluation.

Numerical optimization of low-order Runge, Kutta tableaus collected 2026-09-07 23:25 CT, cycle 2376

Classical construction treats Runge, Kutta order conditions as polynomial constraints and uses remaining tableau parameters to optimize secondary criteria. Ralston minimized specified bounds on leading truncation error for second-, third-, and fourth-order formulas. Modern RK-Opt software similarly supports minimizing the principal error norm, while warning that the resulting constrained problems are highly nonconvex and therefore using many local searches from random starting points. Consequently, algebraic order and stage count alone do not determine the best error behavior within a cell: the selected coefficients and objective norm matter.

RKTK extends this numerical-search approach by efficiently enumerating rooted trees, evaluating Butcher weights and derivatives, and running parallel nonlinear searches over order-condition residuals. Recent work on Williamson-type 2N-storage schemes also shows how structural constraints change the available degrees of freedom: four- and five-stage third-order families can be reduced analytically to free parameters plus a quadratic equation, whereas order four and above generally require numerical solution. These results support searching each order-stage class separately. They do not establish the stated cross-cell comparisons under Q15 floor rounding or cycle-cost models, because the cited optimization objectives concern exact-coefficient order residuals, principal truncation error, stability, or storage, not quantized execution error.

  • Order conditions form a nonlinear polynomial constraint system whose size grows rapidly with order.
  • Free tableau parameters are commonly chosen by minimizing leading truncation-error coefficients or their norm.
  • Different error norms or target problem classes can select different coefficients from the same RK family.
  • RK-Opt uses multistart local optimization because tableau-design problems are highly nonconvex.
  • RKTK computes rooted-tree Butcher weights and gradients for parallel numerical searches over order conditions.
  • Extra stages add coefficients but may also introduce storage or structural constraints.
  • Analytic parameterizations exist for some four- and five-stage third-order 2N-storage families.
  • Exact-arithmetic error constants do not include Q15 coefficient quantization, floor-rounding bias, overflow behavior, or cycle cost.

Quantized Runge, Kutta error under floor rounding collected 2026-09-06 05:40 CT, cycle 1256

Low-precision ODE studies show that rounding behavior can dominate formal discretization order. Fixed-point experiments on neural ODEs found stochastic rounding more robust and accurate than round-to-nearest or truncation across several explicit solvers; reduced stochastic rounding using six residual/random bits retained much of the benefit. Separate Runge, Kutta analysis for a heat-equation discretization found that deterministic round-to-nearest errors were correlated across steps, grew as the timestep decreased until stagnation, while stochastic rounding substantially reduced systematic accumulation. These results do not directly establish behavior for floor rounding, but fixed-point documentation classifies floor as a low-cost mode with large negative bias, making persistent signed drift a specific concern rather than a zero-mean noise assumption.

Runge, Kutta roundoff is also method- and implementation-dependent. Analysis of internal stability shows that stage perturbations can be strongly amplified and that algebraically equivalent implementations of one tableau can have different internal error propagation. Consequently, comparisons among the stated two-, three-, four-, and five-stage cells cannot be inferred from formal order alone: they also depend on where Q15 reductions occur, coefficient quantization, accumulator width, and each tableau's internal amplification under floor rounding. This is especially relevant under a fixed Cortex-M0+ budget because the processor can be configured with either a single-cycle or a 32-cycle multiplier, while software stochastic rounding requires a PRNG plus masks, shifts, and addition; its published accuracy benefit therefore does not imply feasibility at the target cycle limit.

  • Deterministic rounding errors need not be independent between RK stages or timesteps.
  • More stages create additional perturbation sites, but their resulting error depends on internal stability and implementation.
  • Algebraically equivalent implementations of the same RK method can amplify internal errors differently.
  • Stochastic rounding improved fixed-point neural-ODE results across multiple explicit solvers.
  • Six stochastic residual/random bits were sufficient in one neural-ODE study, but software stochastic rounding adds PRNG and instruction costs.
  • Q15 versus wider accumulation changes where quantization occurs; CMSIS-DSP exposes Q15 multiplication, saturation, and wider accumulator operations.

Low-stage RK stability under cycle limits collected 2026-09-06 04:48 CT, cycle 1248

For an s-stage explicit Runge, Kutta method, the stability function is a polynomial of degree at most s, while order p fixes its coefficients through z^p. Consequently, the classical four-stage, fourth-order case has no remaining stability-polynomial freedom, whereas a four-stage, third-order method retains a free fourth-degree coefficient that can be optimized for a target spectrum. Extra stages beyond the order create additional freedom: five- or six-stage methods can enlarge or reshape stability regions, but higher order alone does not guarantee greater stability or lower error per cycle. Stabilized explicit methods show the extreme form of this tradeoff: for spectra near the negative real axis, optimized stability intervals can grow approximately as s², although damping shortens the real interval to give the region nonzero width and stage recurrences must control amplification of roundoff errors.

Stiffness makes an explicit method pay through a stability-limited step size even when accuracy would permit larger steps. Stabilized explicit RK can reduce the required work from scaling linearly with the negative-real spectral radius to roughly its square root, but the literature still characterizes it chiefly as a moderately stiff strategy. Implicit RK removes the bounded explicit stability-region restriction but replaces every step with algebraic solves; it pays when the reduction in step count exceeds the iteration, Jacobian, memory, and solve costs. A recent application found an implicit method cheaper only because good initial guesses kept the average below two operator evaluations per update, while also observing failures when its fixed-point iteration did not converge, so formal A-stability does not make a particular nonlinear solver unconditionally usable.

On Cortex-M0+, stage-count comparisons must use implementation-specific cycle costs. Arm documents a configurable MULS implementation taking either one cycle or 32 cycles, so equal-stage methods with different coefficient-multiplication requirements can have very different budgets. These results support testing the stated order/stage hypotheses against each target spectrum and realized fixed-point instruction schedule: neither the four-stage order comparison nor the five-versus-six-stage comparison has a literature-backed universal winner, and additional stages are beneficial only when their accuracy or stability gain exceeds their full Q15 evaluation cost and internal rounding amplification.

  • An explicit s-stage RK method has a stability polynomial of degree at most s.
  • Order p fixes the stability-polynomial coefficients through degree p.
  • Four-stage order four has no free stability-polynomial coefficient; four-stage order three has one.
  • Stages beyond the order can be used to optimize stability-region shape or error constants.
  • Negative-real stability intervals of stabilized explicit RK methods can grow approximately quadratically with stage count.
  • Damping trades some real-axis interval length for finite stability width around that axis.
  • Internal stage ordering or recurrence affects amplification of roundoff errors.
  • Implicit methods pay off only when saved explicit steps outweigh nonlinear-solve and memory costs.

Classical analysis separates discretization error from arithmetic perturbations. For an order-p one-step method, the modified-equation perturbation begins at order h^p, so increasing order reduces truncation error when h is sufficiently small. This advantage eventually encounters an arithmetic floor: a posteriori analysis under probabilistic roundoff models finds accumulated roundoff proportional to h^(-1/2), while experiments and theory show that reducing the step size can ultimately increase total error. Backward error analysis interprets a numerical trajectory as the exact flow of a perturbed differential equation, but ordinary smooth modified-equation results describe truncation primarily and do not by themselves account for discontinuous Q15 quantization.

Cortex-M0+ multiply variants and CSD constant multiplication collected 2026-09-06 00:52 CT, cycle 1208

Arm specifies two Cortex-M0+ multiplier implementations: a fast single-cycle 32×32 MULS and a smaller iterative implementation taking 32 cycles. The instruction returns the least-significant 32 bits; this is sufficient for a signed Q15-by-Q15 product because the full product of two 16-bit operands fits in 32 bits. Cortex-M0+ implements Armv6-M without the DSP extension, while ordinary register shifts, additions, and subtractions have one-cycle instruction timings under the reference manual's zero-wait-state assumptions. Consequently, a single arithmetic-cost model cannot represent all Cortex-M0+ implementations: on a slow-multiplier part, a short shift-add sequence can undercut MULS, whereas on a fast-multiplier part it generally cannot on instruction count alone.

Canonical signed-digit representation writes a constant using digits −1, 0, and 1 with a minimum number of nonzero digits, turning constant multiplication into shifted operands combined by additions and subtractions. GCC explicitly recognizes Cortex-M0, M0+, and M1 “small-multiply” targets and describes replacing multiplication with add/subtract/shift sequences. CSD reduces the number of signed power-of-two terms, but it does not by itself establish the cheapest complete MCU instruction sequence, which can also depend on constant loading, reusable intermediate values, register pressure, spills, and shifts needed for Q15 rescaling. Arithmetic right shift sign-extends negative values, so applied to a signed product it implements division by a power of two rounded toward negative infinity; that matches floor-style Q15 rescaling, provided the signed-width and overflow assumptions are maintained. These facts support testing the stated Runge-Kutta comparisons separately under fast and slow multiplier models and counting each tableau coefficient's realized instruction sequence rather than treating every coefficient multiplication as one uniform operation.

  • Cortex-M0+ MULS costs either 1 or 32 cycles depending on the implemented multiplier variant.
  • MULS produces the low 32 bits of a 32×32 product.
  • A signed Q15-by-Q15 product fits in signed 32-bit storage before rescaling.
  • Armv6-M Cortex-M0+ has no DSP extension.
  • Reference zero-wait-state timings list register shifts, adds, and subtracts as one-cycle instructions.
  • GCC provides cortex-m0plus.small-multiply tuning for shift/add/substitution of multiplication.
  • CSD minimizes nonzero signed digits, not necessarily total compiled cycles.
  • Arithmetic right shift sign extension gives floor division by powers of two for signed two's-complement values.

Quantized Runge, Kutta rounding on Cortex-M0+ collected 2026-09-05 21:47 CT, cycle 1176

Fixed-point ODE experiments show that arithmetic details can materially change solver error. Hopkins and coauthors evaluated several fixed-step RK2 and RK3 neural ODE solvers and found stochastic rounding consistently more accurate than round-to-nearest fixed point across their tested regimes. Their results also changed when multiplication used round-down and when expressions were reordered to retain wide intermediates longer. Thus, tableau order and stage count alone do not determine realized accuracy under Q15 arithmetic; coefficient quantization, narrowing points, operation order, overflow handling, and rounding mode are part of the implemented method.

Floor rounding is especially relevant because each representable finite result is displaced downward by between zero and one least-significant unit, rather than receiving a zero-mean rounding error. Repeated stage products and state updates can therefore accumulate directional error. Related Runge, Kutta finite-difference analysis found stagnation and global error growth under deterministic round-to-nearest at sufficiently small timesteps, while stochastic rounding made leading errors zero-mean and substantially slowed their accumulation. This challenges comparisons based only on formal orders 2, 4: with Q15 and a fixed cycle budget, extra stages create extra quantization sites, and higher order can lose its expected advantage once roundoff dominates truncation error.

Official CMSIS-DSP documentation confirms that Cortex implementations support Q15 and Q31 arithmetic, commonly form wider products before shifting and saturating back to the destination format, and trade resolution against execution speed. Cortex-M0+ itself may use either a single-cycle or 32-cycle 32-bit multiplier. Consequently, a cycle-constrained search should model the exact target multiplier and explicitly reproduce floor shifts, intermediate widths, saturation, and narrowing placement; stochastic rounding is useful as a bias-control comparison but entails random-number and residual-handling costs absent from deterministic floor arithmetic.

Quantized Runge–Kutta on Cortex-M0+ collected 2026-08-30 17:32 CT, cycle 549

Runge–Kutta roundoff is not merely an independent per-step disturbance: perturbations introduced in stage calculations propagate both within a step and across later steps, and their amplification depends on the RK coefficients and the precise implementation. In Q15 arithmetic, flooring every rescaled product produces an error in the interval from minus one quantization unit up to zero, creating directional bias rather than the cancellation expected from zero-mean errors. Repeated small updates may consequently drift or disappear entirely, while saturation introduces a separate nonlinear error. Wider intermediates and delayed narrowing can reduce the number of quantization points without changing the nominal RK formula.

Research on low-precision Runge–Kutta discretizations of the heat equation found correlated error growth and eventual stagnation under deterministic round-to-nearest, whereas stochastic rounding made leading errors zero-mean and substantially reduced accumulation. Those results do not directly establish performance for general fixed-point ODEs or floor rounding, but they show why a consistently biased rule deserves separate analysis. On Cortex-M0+, stochastic rounding also requires extra state and operations, while multiply latency is implementation-dependent: the core can contain either a single-cycle or a 32-cycle 32-bit multiplier. Thus fixed cycle budgets must be checked against the particular MCU and compiled instruction sequence, not the architecture name alone.

  • Stochastic rounding removes earliest-order rounding bias in expectation but adds random-number and comparison costs.
