Numerical optimization of Runge, Kutta tableaus collected 2026-09-08 20:06 CT, cycle 2584

Runge, Kutta design is naturally a constrained optimization problem: the Butcher coefficients must satisfy polynomial order conditions, while remaining degrees of freedom can optimize leading truncation-error norm, stability, SSP behavior, or storage. RK-Opt supports searches indexed by stage count and order, including minimization of the principal error norm. Its documentation stresses that coefficient optimization is highly nonconvex, so multistart searches can converge to different local solutions and do not automatically certify global optimality. Classical constructions likewise use free parameters and numerical searches to reduce error coefficients; Dormand and Prince’s search over a parameterized family is a documented example.

Extra stages can therefore improve a method even without raising its formal order, but neither order nor stage count alone determines realized error. Higher order suppresses truncation error asymptotically, whereas principal-error optimization addresses only the leading exact-arithmetic term. Internal-error research shows that roundoff or inexact stage computations can be amplified and that amplification depends strongly on the implementation. Consequently, the literature makes an order-4 five-stage advantage over order-2 or order-3 cells plausible in a truncation-dominated regime, but it does not establish H-682 or H-684 through H-687 under Q15 floor rounding and fixed cycle budgets; those comparisons require objectives or validation that explicitly include quantized coefficients, floor-rounded stage arithmetic, implementation form, and the applicable cost model.

RK stability under fixed cycle budgets collected 2026-09-08 19:36 CT, cycle 2576

For an explicit Runge, Kutta method, linear stability is governed by a polynomial whose earliest coefficients are fixed by the requested order and whose remaining coefficients can enlarge the stability region. Recent work formulates method selection as maximizing the admissible step for the actual eigenvalue spectrum. Consequently, stage count and order alone do not establish superiority: a five-stage fourth-order method has fewer free stability-polynomial coefficients than a six-stage second-order method, while a four-stage third-order method can devote its extra coefficient to stability. Runge, Kutta, Chebyshev results show the limiting alternative for diffusion-like spectra: low-order stabilized explicit methods can obtain a negative-real stability interval proportional to the square of the stage count, although their applicability assumes spectra concentrated near the negative real axis. Internal amplification of roundoff is a separate constraint and becomes important in low precision.

Under a fixed cycle budget, the relevant comparison is stable simulated time and achieved error per cycle, not error per step. Extra stages pay when their larger stable step or higher-order accuracy compensates for fewer completed steps; optimized stability must also match whether the problem spectrum is real-negative or substantially complex. Implicit DIRK and ESDIRK methods provide stiffly accurate, L-stable formulas, but each implicit stage requires solving an algebraic system. They pay for themselves only when explicit stability restrictions would otherwise require enough additional steps to exceed those solve costs. That crossover moves against implicit methods on Cortex-M0+, where hardware division is absent and multiplication latency is implementation-dependent; fixed-point nonlinear solves also require convergence tests, scaling, and safeguards. For the stated comparisons, the literature therefore supports measuring spectrum-specific stability extent, internal roundoff amplification under Q15 floor rounding, and total cycles per accepted unit of simulated time alongside held-out error.

Quantized Runge, Kutta rounding and MCU fixed point collected 2026-09-08 19:06 CT, cycle 2568

Runge, Kutta error in low precision contains both discretization error and propagated arithmetic perturbations. General RK analysis shows that roundoff introduced at successive steps can accumulate globally, while reduced-precision analyses give a deterministic roundoff contribution proportional to u/h in addition to the method term h^p. Consequently, decreasing the step size or increasing formal order does not necessarily reduce total error once Q15 quantization dominates. Floor rounding is directed and introduces a non-positive error at every nonexact quantization, so its errors cannot be modeled as unbiased independent noise; repeated stage multiplications and state updates can create systematic drift.

Experiments with fixed-point neural ODE solvers found stochastic rounding more accurate than round-to-nearest fixed point and even binary32 for the studied neuron models and solvers. That work used a fixed-step, second-order Runge, Kutta variant optimized for fixed-point execution and reported that a small random residual was sufficient for stochastic rounding. Related Runge, Kutta heat-equation analysis found that implementation details affect error accumulation and that stochastic rounding can avoid stagnation, although random-number generation adds work and conflicts with a tightly deterministic cycle budget.

For Cortex-M implementations, Q15 stores 15 fractional bits in a signed 16-bit value and Q31 uses a signed 32-bit representation. Arm CMSIS-DSP supplies wider intermediate storage and both saturating and nonsaturating operations, making accumulator width, rescaling points, and saturation behavior part of the numerical method. A five- or six-stage RK cell has more potential quantization sites than a two-stage cell, but the resulting error depends on where values are narrowed and whether products are accumulated at wider precision. Implicit RK cost on small fixed-point MCUs collected 2026-09-08 16:07 CT, cycle 2520

The literature distinguishes three markedly different cost structures. Fully implicit Radau IIA couples all stage equations; the standard three-stage method has order five, but solving its nonlinear stage system is substantially more involved than merely evaluating three right-hand sides. SDIRK makes the tableau lower triangular so stages can be solved sequentially, and its common diagonal coefficient permits reuse of one system-matrix factorization. Rosenbrock methods replace nonlinear stage solves with linear solves; with a common diagonal coefficient, a step typically requires one Jacobian evaluation, one factorization, s right-hand-side evaluations, and s backsolves. Thus stage count alone is not a sufficient cost proxy across these families, although within a fixed implementation each added stage normally adds at least one function evaluation, coefficient accumulation, and solve or backsolve.

A fixed cycle budget favors algorithms whose iteration count and linear-algebra path are statically bounded. Research on implicit RK explicitly supports taking a fixed finite number of Newton or modified-Newton iterations, but preservation of the original order and stability depends on constructing the resulting generalized method accordingly; simply truncating an arbitrary nonlinear iteration has no blanket guarantee. Fixed-point iteration also becomes restrictive for strongly negative Jacobian eigenvalues because convergence can require small step sizes. Rosenbrock methods offer more predictable execution because they prescribe linear solves rather than convergence-controlled nonlinear iteration, while SDIRK can also be bounded by fixing the per-stage iteration count.

These results make comparisons among four-, five-, and six-stage order-four cells dependent on more than nominal stage count. A five-stage method incurs extra arithmetic relative to a four-stage method, but it can still produce lower error if its coefficients, stability behavior, or solver convergence are better; relative to six stages it has a direct opportunity to save one stage's recurring work. On Cortex-M0+, Q15 coefficient products and floor-rounding operations sit inside every stage and iteration, so those recurring counts matter, while Jacobian construction and factorization can dominate for multidimensional implicit methods. The consulted literature does not establish accuracy results specifically for Q15 with floor rounding, so the stated archive comparisons remain empirical questions rather than consequences of classical floating-point order theory.

Wider intermediates and delayed narrowing can reduce the number of quantization points without changing the nominal RK formula.

Research on low-precision Runge–Kutta discretizations of the heat equation found correlated error growth and eventual stagnation under deterministic round-to-nearest, whereas stochastic rounding made leading errors zero-mean and substantially reduced accumulation. Those results do not directly establish performance for general fixed-point ODEs or floor rounding, but they show why a consistently biased rule deserves separate analysis. On Cortex-M0+, stochastic rounding also requires extra state and operations, while multiply latency is implementation-dependent: the core can contain either a single-cycle or a 32-cycle 32-bit multiplier. Thus fixed cycle budgets must be checked against the particular MCU and compiled instruction sequence, not the architecture name alone.

  • Floor quantization has a one-sided error and therefore introduces systematic negative bias.
  • RK stage perturbations can be amplified within a single step, depending on coefficients and implementation form.
  • Accumulated perturbations can dominate truncation error as the step size is reduced.
  • Small quantized increments can vanish, causing stagnation or frozen state components.
  • Stochastic rounding removes earliest-order rounding bias in expectation but adds random-number and comparison costs.
  • Q15 uses 16-bit storage with 15 fractional bits; wider intermediates can postpone narrowing and saturation.
  • Saturation and rounding are distinct error mechanisms and should be measured separately.
  • Cortex-M0+ implementations may have either one-cycle or 32-cycle multiplication, materially affecting cycle estimates.
